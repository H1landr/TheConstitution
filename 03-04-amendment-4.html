Part Three · The Bill of Rights

Amendment 4 — Searches and Seizures

Ratified 1791 The traffic-stop amendment 6 min read

In the Text

Plain English

Two clauses. The first bans unreasonable searches and seizures. The second says what a warrant has to contain. They are not the same rule, and the difference matters.

Clause one: unreasonable

The amendment protects your person, your home, your papers, and your belongings. It does not ban searches. It bans unreasonable ones — a word the text never defines.

Courts filled that gap in 1967 with a standard that has caused trouble ever since: a search happens when the government intrudes on a reasonable expectation of privacy. You expect privacy in a closed bathroom stall. You do not expect it in a public park.

That test is workable for physical spaces and awkward for digital ones. A phone in your pocket holds more private information than your house did in 1791. Courts have had to decide, case by case, how far the old categories stretch.

Clause two: warrants

A warrant is a judge's permission slip. To get one, police must show probable cause — a reasonable basis to believe evidence or contraband will be found. They must swear to it, and the warrant must say specifically where they will look and what they are looking for.

That last requirement is a direct response to the writs of assistance, the general search warrants British officers used in the colonies. Those warrants named no place and no item. They authorized searching anything, anywhere. The Fourth Amendment was written to make that kind of warrant impossible.

Most searches happen without a warrant

This surprises people. The Supreme Court has recognized many exceptions where police may search without asking a judge first.

They may search you when you are arrested, to find weapons and prevent destruction of evidence. They may search a car they have probable cause to believe contains contraband, because a car can drive away. They may act without a warrant in a genuine emergency. They may take what is in plain sight. They may stop and frisk someone on reasonable suspicion of a weapon, which is a lower standard than probable cause.

You can also consent. Most searches in the United States happen because someone said yes to a request. You are generally allowed to say no, and the police are generally not required to tell you so.

The exclusionary rule

The amendment says nothing about what happens when it is violated. The courts supplied an answer: evidence obtained through an illegal search is usually excluded from trial.

This is called the exclusionary rule. Its logic is blunt. Without it, the amendment would have no enforcement mechanism, and police would have no reason to follow it.

The rule is controversial and has been narrowed repeatedly. There are exceptions for good-faith mistakes, for evidence that would inevitably have been found anyway, and for some collateral uses of the evidence.

Why It Matters

For most Americans, the Fourth Amendment is the one they will actually encounter. The First Amendment matters when you speak in public. This one matters at a traffic stop, a border crossing, a knock at the door.

Its central weakness is that almost every rule is judge-made. The text gives a principle — reasonableness — and no instructions. Whether a search is lawful turns on decades of decisions about expectation of privacy, warrant exceptions, and standing, none of which is in the document.

The newest frontier is data. Courts have held that police generally need a warrant to get cell-site location records, because a phone's movement over months reveals more than any single search could. But the same information shared voluntarily with a company has historically received less protection. That tension has not been resolved.
