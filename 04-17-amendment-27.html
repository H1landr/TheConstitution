Part Four · The Amendments Since 1791

Amendment 27 — Congressional Pay

Ratified 1992 202 years in the making 4 min read

In the Text

Plain English

If Congress votes itself a pay raise, the raise cannot take effect until after the next House election. Voters get a chance to react first.

Where it came from

This was one of twelve amendments Congress proposed on September 25, 1789 — the same package that produced the Bill of Rights. Ten were ratified by 1791. Two were not.

One of the two failed amendments concerned the number of representatives. The other was this one, about congressional pay. It sat unratified for two centuries. Most people assumed it was dead.

The student who revived it

In 1982, a student at the University of Texas named Gregory Watson wrote a paper arguing the amendment was still alive. His point was that Article V sets no deadline, and Congress had not attached one in 1789.

His professor gave the paper a C, on the grounds that the argument was implausible.

Watson spent the next decade writing to state legislatures. Maine ratified in 1983, and others followed slowly. By 1992, thirty-eight states had approved it — the threshold required by Article V — and the amendment became part of the Constitution, 202 years after it was proposed. Watson's grade was later changed.

Why no deadline matters

Article V does not require ratification within any particular time. Congress often adds a deadline when it proposes an amendment — seven years is typical — as it did for the Equal Rights Amendment.

In 1789, Congress attached no deadline to this one. That is the technical reason the amendment survived. Whether a ratification ratified across two centuries should count is a fair question. The answer the country settled on was yes.

Why It Matters

Two things about this amendment are worth carrying away.

The first is that the amendment process is stranger than its reputation. It is not a single act at a single moment. It is a procedure with gaps built into it, and one of those gaps stayed open long enough for a college student to walk through it.

The second is a bookkeeping point with real consequences. If this amendment was valid, then there were twelve amendments proposed in 1789, not ten. The Bill of Rights is amendments one through ten because two of the original twelve failed at the time — and one of them came back.

It also closes this part of the document on a fitting note. The last amendment ratified was the first one proposed. The Constitution has not stopped moving, and the oldest text in it was the most recent to take effect.
