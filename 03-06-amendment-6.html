Part Three · The Bill of Rights

Amendment 6 — The Right to a Fair Trial

Ratified 1791 Six guarantees 6 min read

In the Text

Plain English

This amendment describes what a fair trial actually requires, step by step. It is the most concrete provision in the Bill of Rights.

Speedy

A person accused but not tried sits in limbo — possibly in jail, possibly with a case hanging over their life for years. Delay favors the prosecution, because memories fade and witnesses disappear.

There is no fixed number of days. Courts weigh four things: how long the delay was, why it happened, whether the defendant objected, and whether the delay caused harm. Delays of several years have been upheld when the defense caused them, and shorter delays have been struck down when the government was responsible.

Public

Trials happen in open court, with the public and press able to attend. The point is not transparency for its own sake. A trial conducted in secret can become a proceeding where nobody outside can tell whether the rules were followed.

Closure is permitted occasionally — to protect a child witness, for example, or to prevent publicity that would poison the jury pool. It is the exception, and courts require a specific finding to allow it.

An impartial jury

The jury comes from the state and district where the crime happened, and it must be impartial. In practice this creates two obligations.

First, the pool must be drawn from a fair cross-section of the community, not from a hand-picked list.

Second, both sides may strike a limited number of jurors without giving a reason — peremptory challenges. But those strikes cannot be used to remove jurors because of race or sex. Prosecutors who strike all the Black jurors in a case must offer a neutral explanation.

Informed of the charge

You must be told what you are accused of and on what basis. This sounds obvious. It is the difference between defending yourself against a specific allegation and guessing at what the government has in mind.

Confronting witnesses

The prosecution's witnesses must testify in court, where the defense can cross-examine them. A written statement from an absent witness is generally not enough.

The Supreme Court tightened this rule in 2004. If a statement is testimonial, and made to police during an investigation, the witness must generally appear. The exception is when they are genuinely unavailable and the defendant had a prior chance to cross-examine them.

The clause also protects against the classic abuse of a secret accuser whose identity and story the defense never gets to test.

Compulsory process

You can force witnesses to appear on your behalf. The court can issue a subpoena, and a reluctant witness who ignores one can be held in contempt.

Without this, the right to call witnesses would depend on whether they felt like showing up — and the government could pressure them not to.

A lawyer

The last clause contains the most consequential development in modern criminal procedure.

For most of American history, this meant you could hire a lawyer if you could afford one. In 1963, in Gideon v. Wainwright, the Supreme Court held that states must provide a lawyer to a defendant charged with a serious crime who cannot pay. Clarence Gideon had been convicted of breaking into a poolroom in Florida and had asked for counsel. He was refused, and he appealed his conviction by hand from a prison cell.

The rule expanded over the following decade to cover any case where jail time is imposed. The result is the public defender system — underfunded almost everywhere, and the subject of a long-running argument about whether the right is real in practice.

Why It Matters

The Sixth Amendment is where the Constitution puts its weight behind one idea. A trial is a contest between equals, not an exercise of government power against a lone person.

Most of its guarantees cost money and slow things down. That is the point. Speedy trials, public proceedings, cross-examination, and paid lawyers all make prosecution harder. A system that skipped them would convict more people and convict more innocent ones.

The gap between the right on paper and the right in practice is the live issue. A lawyer with 150 cases and no investigator is technically counsel. Whether that satisfies the amendment is a question courts have mostly declined to answer.
