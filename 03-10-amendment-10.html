Part Three · The Bill of Rights

Amendment 10 — Reserved Powers

Ratified 1791 One sentence 5 min read

In the Text

Plain English

This is the federalism amendment, and it is the most frequently misunderstood sentence in the Bill of Rights.

Read plainly, it says: Congress has the powers the Constitution gives it. Everything else belongs to the states or to the people. That is a reasonable restatement of how the system was designed.

The word that is missing

The Articles of Confederation said powers not expressly delegated were kept by the states. The Tenth Amendment drops expressly.

That single omission is the whole story. If only express powers belonged to Congress, then implied powers — like the national bank, or federal agencies, or most of modern government — would be unconstitutional. By removing the word, the framers left room for Congress to do things the text does not spell out.

It was not an accident. The framers had just spent a decade under a government that could only do what was written down, and it did not work.

What the amendment is not

It is not a general limit on federal power. It is a rule about how to read the document, not a grant of authority to anyone.

The Supreme Court made this point bluntly in 1985. The Tenth Amendment, it said, states a truism: whatever is not given to the federal government is kept by the states. It does not tell courts how to decide what has been given.

So if Congress passes a law under a power it clearly has, the Tenth Amendment does not block it. It only matters if Congress lacked the power in the first place.

Courts have also refused to strike down federal laws simply because they regulate states. In 1985 the Court upheld a federal law setting a minimum drinking age by withholding highway money from states that refused. The decision was a blow to anyone hoping the Tenth Amendment would shield states from federal pressure.

Where the protection actually lives

If you want to know why the federal government cannot simply order states to do things, the answer is not mainly the Tenth Amendment. It is a set of doctrines the Court built elsewhere.

The Court has held that Congress cannot commandeer a state's legislature or its executive officers to carry out federal programs. It has held that Congress cannot make states waive sovereign immunity in their own courts. Both doctrines protect state autonomy, and neither is grounded in the Tenth Amendment's text.

There is also the Spending Clause, which is where the real action is now. The federal government raises money and offers it to states with conditions attached. That is coercive in effect and lawful in form, and it is how a great deal of federal policy gets implemented.

Why It Matters

The Tenth Amendment is invoked constantly in political argument and almost never decides a case. It expresses an idea the country cares about — that states are not administrative districts of the federal government — without giving judges a lever to enforce it.

That gap is instructive. The framers did write down a rule about the balance of power. Then two centuries of interpretation moved the balance, and the written rule turned out to be too abstract to stop the movement.

It is worth comparing with the Ninth Amendment, which faces the same problem from the opposite direction. One says there are rights we did not list. The other says there are powers we did not delegate. Both are true statements that leave the hard work undone, and the hard work has always been done by courts.
