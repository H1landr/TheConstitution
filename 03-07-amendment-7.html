Part Three · The Bill of Rights

Amendment 7 — Civil Juries

Ratified 1791 The amendment nobody remembers 4 min read

In the Text

Plain English

The Sixth Amendment protects juries in criminal cases. This one protects them in civil cases — lawsuits between private parties, where nobody goes to jail.

That distinction is the whole amendment. If someone sues you over a car crash, a broken contract, or an injury on their property, you generally have the right to a jury. Twelve ordinary people decide the facts, not a single judge.

The twenty dollars

The text sets the threshold at twenty dollars, which was a meaningful sum in 1791. Congress has never adjusted it for inflation, and it cannot easily do so without amending the Constitution. The result is that the requirement now applies to essentially every civil suit, since twenty dollars is worth far less today than it was when the amendment was written.

This is a small, honest example of how a constitution ages. A number that once filtered out trivial cases now filters out nothing.

The second clause

No fact tried by a jury may be re-examined by another federal court except according to common law rules. In ordinary language: an appeals court can review the judge's legal rulings, but it cannot second-guess what the jury decided the facts were.

That matters more than it sounds. In most legal systems, an appellate court can revisit findings of fact. Here, the jury's factual conclusion is close to final, which is a significant transfer of authority from judges to citizens.

What it does not do

The Seventh Amendment has never been applied to the states. This is one of the few provisions the Supreme Court has declined to incorporate. Its reasoning is that civil juries are not fundamental to a fair trial in the way a criminal jury is.

So the right exists in federal court and does not exist as a constitutional matter in state court. Most states do provide civil juries anyway, by their own constitutions or by statute. But they are not required to.

Why It Matters

This amendment is nearly invisible in constitutional law and quite important in ordinary life. Civil juries are the main mechanism by which ordinary people pass judgment on corporations, landlords, hospitals, and insurers.

They are also, correspondingly, under pressure. Mandatory arbitration clauses are the fine print you agree to when you open an account or buy a phone. They routinely move disputes out of court and away from juries entirely. None of that is unconstitutional. It is a private agreement. But it accomplishes what the amendment was meant to prevent, and it has happened without anyone amending anything.

That is worth noticing. A right can be preserved in the text and hollowed out in practice, and the Constitution has no obvious remedy for it.
