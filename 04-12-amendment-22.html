Part Four · The Amendments Since 1791

Amendment 22 — Two Terms

Ratified 1951 A limit on the voters 4 min read

In the Text

Plain English

Nobody can be elected president more than twice. The second half of the sentence handles the awkward case of someone who took over mid-term.

How the math works

If a vice president takes over and serves more than two years of the predecessor's term, they may only be elected once afterward. So their total service can be about six years.

If they serve two years or less, they may be elected twice. That works out to a maximum of roughly ten years in office.

The amendment limits elections, not years. It is possible, in theory, to serve longer than ten years through some combination the text does not address. That has never happened.

Why it passed

Because of Franklin Roosevelt. He won four elections — 1932, 1936, 1940, and 1944 — breaking a tradition that every previous president had followed since George Washington stepped down after two terms.

Washington's decision was not law. It was custom, and it held for 144 years. Roosevelt's fourth victory made the custom look fragile, and Congress proposed this amendment in 1947, two years after he died in office.

The amendment included a carve-out. It did not apply to the person holding the office when it was proposed, which was Harry Truman. Truman was eligible to run again in 1952 and chose not to.

The argument against it

Term limits are unusual in a democracy. They tell voters they are not allowed to choose someone they want to choose.

The case for the amendment is that a president who never faces voters again becomes a lame duck immediately. It is also that a long-serving president accumulates enough power to distort the system. The case against is that it removes experience at exactly the moment a country might need it. And it substitutes a rule for a judgment voters are capable of making.

Repeal proposals have been introduced repeatedly. None has come close.

Why It Matters

This is the amendment that limits the people rather than the government. Every other restriction in the document constrains what officials may do. This one constrains who citizens may vote for.

It is also a reminder that the Constitution is not only a set of protections. It is a design, and designs encode judgments about how much power any one person should hold over time. The framers of 1787 did not think a term limit was necessary. The generation of 1947 did.

Which version was right is not a question the text can answer. It is a question about how much you trust voters, and how much you fear concentration of power. Reasonable people land in different places.
