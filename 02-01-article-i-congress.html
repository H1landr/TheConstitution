Part Two · The Seven Articles

Article I — Congress

Ten sections The longest article 9 min read

In the Text

That is the opening sentence, and it is doing three separate jobs at once. Article I is the longest of the seven articles by a wide margin, because the men in Philadelphia expected Congress to be where most of the action was. It runs ten sections, and the plain-English walkthrough below takes them in order.

Plain English

1Two houses, and one place where laws come from

Lawmaking power belongs to Congress — not to the president, and not to the courts. Congress is split into two chambers, a Senate and a House of Representatives, and both have to agree before anything becomes law.

The load-bearing phrase is herein granted. Congress does not have whatever powers it can grab. It has the powers this document lists, and that is the end of the list. That single restriction is the foundation of the whole American system of limited government.

2The House of Representatives

Members of the House serve two-year terms — the shortest term anywhere in the federal government. The entire House faces the voters every two years. The founders designed it that way on purpose: the chamber closest to the people should be the one that has to answer to them most often.

To serve, you have to be at least 25 years old, a citizen for seven years, and a resident of the state you represent. Seats are divided among the states by population, which is why a census happens every ten years.

And here the document has to be read honestly, because this section contains one of the worst bargains in American history. When counting people for representation, the text says to count three fifths of all other Persons — a phrase that meant enslaved people. They were counted as three-fifths of a human being for the purpose of giving their enslavers more seats in Congress, and they were given no rights at all. The word “slavery” does not appear; the delegates avoided it. The Fourteenth Amendment later replaced this counting rule, and the Thirteenth abolished the practice it was built around.

The House also gets the sole Power of Impeachment — meaning only the House can formally charge a federal official with misconduct. It is the indictment, not the trial.

3The Senate

Two senators per state, regardless of size — so Wyoming gets the same Senate votes as California. Senators serve six-year terms, must be at least 30, and must have been citizens for nine years.

The Senate was built to be the slower, steadier chamber. Only about a third of it is up for election in any two-year cycle, so the body never turns over all at once.

The vice president presides over the Senate but votes only to break a tie. That is why the job is sometimes described as having no real power except on the days it has all of it.

The Senate holds the sole Power to try all Impeachments — it conducts the trial after the House brings charges. Conviction takes two-thirds of the senators present, and the only punishments the Senate can impose are removal from office and a ban on holding office again. A separate criminal trial can still follow.

One change worth knowing: originally, state legislatures chose their senators. The Seventeenth Amendment, in 1913, handed that choice to the voters directly.

4Elections and meetings

States run their own elections — they set the times, places, and rules — but Congress can override those rules by law. Congress must also meet at least once a year.

That override exists for a practical reason. If a state could simply refuse to hold an election, it could quietly empty its own seats in Congress. Section 4 closes that door.

5How Congress runs itself

Each chamber is the final judge of its own elections and qualifications. A quorum — the minimum number of members who must be present to do business — is a majority. Each chamber writes its own rules, can punish members for disorderly conduct, and can expel a member with a two-thirds vote.

Each chamber must also keep a public journal of what it does, except for parts it decides need secrecy. And if one-fifth of the members present ask for it, the individual yes and no votes get recorded by name. That rule is how you can look up exactly how your representative voted.

6Pay, and the Speech or Debate Clause

Members are paid from the federal Treasury. Beyond that, they get two protections.

First, they generally cannot be arrested while traveling to or from a session, with narrow exceptions for treason, serious crimes, and breaches of the peace.

Second — and this one matters much more — for any Speech or Debate in either House, members shall not be questioned in any other Place. A senator cannot be sued or prosecuted for what they say during floor debate. The point is to make sure a member can criticize the president, the military, or a powerful industry without fearing a lawsuit designed to silence them.

Section 6 also blocks a common form of corruption. Members cannot hold other federal offices at the same time. And they cannot be appointed to a new office that they themselves helped create and fund during their term.

7How a bill becomes a law

This is the section most people half-remember from a schoolhouse diagram. In order:

  • Bills raising revenue must start in the House. The Senate can amend them.
  • A bill passes both chambers and goes to the president.
  • The president signs it, or returns it with written objections.
  • If returned, the chamber that started it reconsiders. If two-thirds of each chamber vote to pass it anyway, it becomes law over the president's objection. That is a veto override.
  • If the president does nothing for ten days while Congress is in session, the bill becomes law automatically.
  • But if Congress adjourns during those ten days, the bill dies. This is the pocket veto, and it is the one part of the process the president can use without leaving a written record.

8The eighteen powers

Section 8 is the engine room. It lists, in eighteen numbered clauses, what Congress may actually do. The major ones, in ordinary language:

  • Tax and spend. Collect taxes to pay debts and provide for defence and the general welfare. Duties must be the same in every state.
  • Borrow money.
  • Regulate commerce with foreign nations, among the states, and with Native tribes. This one clause has grown into the basis for a huge share of federal law.
  • Set rules for citizenship and bankruptcy.
  • Coin money, set its value, and fix weights and measures.
  • Punish counterfeiting.
  • Run post offices and post roads.
  • Grant patents and copyrights for limited times, to encourage invention and writing.
  • Create federal courts below the Supreme Court.
  • Punish piracy and crimes at sea.
  • Declare war and set rules for captured property.
  • Raise armies — but no army budget may last more than two years, so a standing army always needs fresh approval.
  • Build and maintain a navy.
  • Write rules for the military.
  • Call up the militia to enforce laws, put down insurrections, and repel invasions.
  • Organize and arm the militia.
  • Govern the federal district — the ten-mile square that became Washington, D.C.
  • Make all laws necessary and proper for carrying out the rest.

That last clause deserves its own paragraph, because it is the most argued-over sentence in Article I. The necessary and proper clause says Congress can pass laws that are needed to carry out its listed powers. Read narrowly, it just lets Congress do the obvious follow-up work. Read broadly, it lets Congress do almost anything that serves a listed goal — and that broader reading is the one courts have generally accepted since 1819, when McCulloch v. Maryland upheld a national bank that Article I never mentions.

9Things Congress may not do

Section 9 is the list of limits on Congress. A few stand out:

  • No banning the slave trade before 1808. Another compromise with slavery, written directly into the text.
  • No suspending habeas corpus — the right to have a court check whether your imprisonment is lawful — except during rebellion or invasion, when public safety requires it.
  • No bills of attainder. Congress cannot simply declare a specific person guilty of a crime and punish them without a trial.
  • No ex post facto laws. You cannot be punished for something that was legal when you did it.
  • No titles of nobility. No dukes, no earls, no hereditary rank.
  • No money out of the Treasury except through a legal appropriation. Congress controls the purse, not the president.

10Things the states may not do

Section 10 applies many of the same limits to state governments, plus a few of its own. States may not make treaties, coin money, print their own paper currency, pass bills of attainder or ex post facto laws, or grant titles of nobility.

It also contains one sentence that shaped a century of American law: no state may pass a law impairing the Obligation of Contracts. During the Depression, that clause was used repeatedly to strike down state laws protecting homeowners from foreclosure. The Supreme Court changed course in 1934 and allowed such laws to stand.

Finally, states may not keep troops or warships in peacetime, or make agreements with foreign countries, without Congress's consent.

Why It Matters

Article I is long because the founders expected Congress to run the country. They had just spent a decade under a system with no president and a Congress that could not tax, and it had failed. So they wrote the powers of the legislature out in detail, and left the other branches comparatively short.

What actually happened was different. Over the past century, real power has drifted steadily toward the president and toward federal agencies — through wars, through delegation, and through sheer size. Congress still passes the laws, but it increasingly sets broad goals and lets others fill in the specifics.

So Article I is worth reading for two reasons at once. It tells you how the system was designed to work, and by comparison it tells you how the system has drifted. Almost every current argument about presidential power is really an argument about whether that drift was legitimate.
