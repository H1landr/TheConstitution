Part Four · The Amendments Since 1791

Amendment 14 — Citizenship and Equality

Ratified 1868 Five sections 7 min read

In the Text

Three sentences. They have produced more lawsuits, more Supreme Court decisions, and more social change than the rest of the document combined. The amendment has four more sections after this one, covering representation, who may hold office, war debts, and enforcement.

Plain English

What the law looked like before 1868

Two things were true before this amendment, and both are easy to miss.

First, the Constitution did not say who was a citizen. It mentioned citizenship in passing, but left the definition to the states. In 1857, the Supreme Court used that gap in Dred Scott v. Sandford to rule that Black Americans — enslaved or free — could never be citizens and had no rights a white person was bound to respect. That decision is widely considered the worst in the Court's history.

Second, and less obvious: the Bill of Rights did not apply to the states. It only limited the federal government. In 1833, in Barron v. Baltimore, the Court said so plainly. So if your state passed a law suppressing speech or conducting warrantless searches, the First and Fourth Amendments had nothing to say about it.

The Fourteenth Amendment fixed both problems in one stroke.

Sentence one: who is a citizen

If you are born here, you are a citizen. That is the rule, and it overruled Dred Scott directly. It is called birthright citizenship, and it is unusual — most countries grant citizenship by descent instead.

The qualifying phrase is subject to the jurisdiction of the United States. Courts have read this to exclude a narrow group: the children of foreign diplomats, who are outside American legal authority by international agreement. The Supreme Court upheld birthright citizenship for a child born to Chinese immigrant parents in 1898, in United States v. Wong Kim Ark.

Whether that phrase excludes anyone else has been argued about for over a century, and it is being argued about now. It is a genuine legal dispute, not a settled one, and this guide is not going to pretend otherwise.

Sentence two, part one: privileges or immunities

Many historians believe this clause was meant to be the big one — a general guarantee that states could not violate the fundamental rights of Americans. If it had worked that way, it would have applied the whole Bill of Rights to the states all at once.

It did not work that way. In 1873, just five years later, the Supreme Court read the clause very narrowly in a group of cases known as the Slaughter-House Cases. The Court said the clause protected only a small set of rights that came from being a national citizen, such as the right to travel between states. It did not protect the ordinary rights people held against their own state government.

The result is strange. The clause is still in the Constitution, but it has almost no practical force. It is the clearest example in the document of a provision whose meaning was decided by interpretation rather than by wording.

Sentence two, part two: due process

This is the clause that actually did the work. It says states cannot take life, liberty, or property without due process of law — meaning fair, established legal procedure rather than a decision made on the spot.

The Supreme Court spent roughly sixty years using this sentence to apply most of the Bill of Rights to state and local governments. It worked one provision at a time, from the 1920s to the 1960s. The process is called incorporation.

Freedom of speech, freedom of religion, the right to a lawyer, protection against self-incrimination, protection against unreasonable searches. All of these now bind your town police department because of this clause. They were not originally limits on your state at all.

Notice the word choice too: any person, not any citizen. Due process protects people who are in the country, whatever their citizenship status.

Sentence two, part three: equal protection

Thirteen words, and the basis of the civil rights movement.

Equal protection means a state cannot apply its laws unequally to different groups of people without a good legal reason. What counts as a good reason is the whole argument. Courts apply stricter scrutiny when a law treats people differently because of race or religion. They apply looser scrutiny when a law draws ordinary practical lines, like a speed limit.

Three cases show the range:

  • Brown v. Board of Education (1954) — racially segregated public schools violate equal protection, overruling the “separate but equal” rule that had stood since 1896.
  • Loving v. Virginia (1967) — a state cannot ban marriage between people of different races.
  • Obergefell v. Hodges (2015) — states must license and recognize marriages between two people of the same sex.

One wrinkle: the text says No State, and the federal government is not a state. The Supreme Court extended equal protection to the federal government in 1954 by treating it as part of the Fifth Amendment's guarantee of due process. That reading is well established, but it is not in the text, and it is worth knowing the difference.

The other four sections

Section 2 punishes states that deny the vote by reducing their seats in the House. It was aimed at the South's suppression of Black voters, and it has never once been enforced.

Section 3 bars from office anyone who swore to support the Constitution and then engaged in insurrection or rebellion — unless Congress votes by two-thirds to lift the bar. It was used against former Confederates, went dormant for a century and a half, and returned to public argument after January 6, 2021. Whether it applies without an act of Congress is contested, and courts have split on it.

Section 4 says the federal war debt is valid. It also says that neither the federal government nor any state may pay debts owed to the Confederacy. Nor may they compensate enslavers for the loss of enslaved people. It guarantees the public debt generally, which is why arguments about the debt ceiling tend to run through this section.

Section 5 gives Congress the power to enforce the amendment by legislation. Most of the great civil rights statutes — the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Voting Rights Act of 1965 — rest on this sentence.

Why It Matters

The original Constitution protected Americans from their federal government. It said almost nothing about protecting them from their own state — and in 1789, the state was the government most people actually dealt with.

The Fourteenth Amendment closed that gap. It made national citizenship primary and put the states under the same basic limits as the federal government. And it gave the country a single sentence — equal protection — that could be used to argue about fairness in court for the next century and a half.

It is also a useful lesson in how the Constitution actually changes. The words on the page were written by people in 1866 who could not have predicted Brown or Obergefell. What the amendment means today depends heavily on decisions made by judges in the decades since. That is why the fight over who sits on courts is a fight about this amendment more than any other.
