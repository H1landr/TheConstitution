Part Four · The Amendments Since 1791

Amendment 23 — Washington, D.C.

Ratified 1961 A partial fix 4 min read

In the Text

Plain English

Washington, D.C. is not a state. It has no senators and no voting representative, and before 1961 its residents could not vote for president either.

This amendment gave them electoral votes. It did not make them a state, and it did not give them a seat in Congress.

The formula

D.C. gets the number of electors it would have if it were a state, but no more than the least populous state. The least populous state has three. So D.C. has three, and always will.

That cap is doing real work. D.C. has about 700,000 residents, more than Wyoming or Vermont, and under the ordinary formula it might have more than three electors. The cap reassured small states that D.C. would never outweigh them.

What residents still do not have

D.C. has one delegate in the House who can speak, serve on committees, and introduce bills — but cannot vote on the floor. It has no senators at all.

So roughly 700,000 American citizens pay federal taxes, serve on juries, and can be drafted, with no voting representation in Congress. The license plates in D.C. say “Taxation Without Representation,” which is a deliberate echo of the 1770s.

Why not just make it a state?

Because Article I says the seat of government is a federal district, governed by Congress, not a state. Turning most of it into a state would require either a constitutional amendment or an act of Congress that shrinks the district and admits the rest.

The second route has been attempted and is contested. Whether Congress can do it by statute, or whether it requires an amendment, is genuinely disputed. The House has passed statehood bills; the Senate has not.

Why It Matters

The Twenty-Third Amendment is a good example of a half-solution. It fixed the most glaring problem — people in the capital could not vote for the president who governed them — while leaving the larger one untouched.

It also shows how the structure of the original document constrains what an amendment can do. The District Clause did not just fail to give D.C. representation. It created a category of place that is not a state and cannot easily become one.

That is a recurring pattern. The Constitution creates categories, and people who fall outside them have to be brought in by amendment, one at a time. D.C. residents waited 172 years for a presidential vote. They are still waiting for a congressional one.
