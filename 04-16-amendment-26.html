Part Four · The Amendments Since 1791

Amendment 26 — Voting at 18

Ratified 1971 Fastest amendment ever 4 min read

In the Text

Plain English

This one breaks from the pattern of the Fifteenth, Nineteenth, and Twenty-Fourth. Those said you cannot be excluded for a particular reason. This one creates a floor: at eighteen, you can vote.

The argument

The slogan was simple: old enough to fight, old enough to vote.

During the Vietnam War, men were drafted at eighteen. They could be sent to combat, and they could not vote in most states. That contradiction became politically unsustainable as casualties rose and campus protests grew.

Why an amendment was needed

Congress tried to fix it by statute first. In 1970 it passed a law lowering the voting age to eighteen in all elections, state and federal.

The Supreme Court struck part of it down in 1970. Congress could set the age for federal elections, the Court said, but not for state elections. That produced an absurd result: eighteen-year-olds could vote for president and Congress but not for governor or state legislature.

States would have had to run two separate voting systems. Nobody wanted that, so the amendment became the practical route.

The speed

Congress proposed it in March 1971. It was ratified on July 1, 1971 — 100 days later. That is the fastest ratification of any amendment, and it is not close.

Eleven states ratified it on the same day the Senate passed it. Five more followed within a week.

Why so fast? Because the alternative was chaos at the ballot box, and because almost nobody wanted to be on record opposing the vote for soldiers.

Why It Matters

This amendment is the counterexample to everything the earlier pages say about how hard amendment is. Article V is slow and deliberately frustrating — until it isn't.

The difference is consensus. Almost every other amendment in this part of the document was fought over for years because it settled a live dispute. This one did not settle a dispute. It cleaned up a mess the Supreme Court had created, in a situation where nearly everyone agreed on the outcome.

That is the pattern worth remembering. Article V is hard when the country is divided and easy when it is not. A hundred days or two hundred years — the amendment process responds to politics, not to the merits.
