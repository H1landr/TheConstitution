Part Three · The Bill of Rights

Amendment 2 — Arms

Ratified 1791 Most disputed amendment 6 min read

In the Text

Plain English

Twenty-seven words, two clauses, and the longest-running argument in American constitutional law. The structure of the sentence is the argument.

The first clause is a statement of purpose: a well regulated militia is necessary to the security of a free state. The second clause is the rule: the right of the people to keep and bear arms shall not be infringed.

If the first clause limits the second, the amendment protects a militia-related right. If it merely explains a reason, the second clause stands on its own as an individual right. That is the whole dispute, and it is genuinely a dispute. Serious lawyers and historians read the sentence both ways.

One word that gets misread

Well regulated does not mean “heavily regulated by law” in eighteenth-century usage. It meant well-organized, properly equipped, and drilled — in working order.

Both sides cite this, which is a sign of how slippery it is. Supporters of gun rights argue that a militia in working order is one where people own their own weapons. Supporters of regulation argue that the phrase shows the framers were thinking about organized, government-managed forces rather than individuals.

What the courts have said

For most of American history, the Supreme Court said almost nothing. Then in 2008, in District of Columbia v. Heller, it decided the question 5–4.

Heller held that the amendment protects an individual right to keep a firearm for traditionally lawful purposes, and that this right is not connected to service in a militia. The case involved a Washington, D.C. ban on handguns in the home. The Court struck it down.

The opinion was careful in one respect that is often overlooked. It said the right is not unlimited, and it listed restrictions it treated as presumptively lawful. These included bans on possession by felons and the mentally ill, and laws forbidding guns in sensitive places like schools and government buildings.

Two years later, McDonald v. City of Chicago applied the right to state and local governments, which it had never done before.

Then in 2022, New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen changed the method courts use. Instead of asking whether a gun law serves an important government interest, courts must now ask whether the regulation is consistent with the nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation. That test is being applied case by case, and the results are still arriving.

Why the history is contested

The amendment came out of a specific problem. The Constitution let Congress create a standing army, and several states did not trust standing armies. They wanted to rely on state militias instead — part-time citizen soldiers. The amendment was a promise that the federal government would not disarm those militias.

What that promise means for an individual living in a city in 2026 is not something the text answers. It requires a judgment about history, purpose, and how much the world has changed since 1791. Back then the country had no standing police forces, and most adult men were expected to muster with their own weapons.

Why It Matters

This is the clearest case in the document of a provision whose meaning stayed unsettled for over two hundred years. Then it was settled by a one-vote margin. Then the method was unsettled again. In 2007, the individual-right reading was the minority position among legal scholars. In 2008 it became the law.

That is worth sitting with. It does not mean the Court was wrong. It means the Constitution does not resolve this question by itself, and anyone who tells you it obviously does is skipping the hard part.

What can be said with confidence is narrower. The amendment protects something real. It does not protect everything. And where the line falls — waiting periods, age limits, concealed carry, assault weapon bans, magazine limits — is being worked out in courts right now.
