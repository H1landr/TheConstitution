Article III — The Courts

Three sections The shortest article 8 min read

In the Text

Plain English

The shortest article

Article III is about 380 words — shorter than Article I, shorter than Article II, shorter than several individual amendments.

The founders spent far less time on the courts than on the other two branches, and it shows. They were not sure federal courts would matter much. In 1787, most legal business happened in state courts, and the framers assumed that would continue.

Instead, Article III became the foundation for the most powerful unelected institution in American government. That happened because of something the article never says.

Section 1 — One Supreme Court, and as many others as Congress wants

The Constitution requires one Supreme Court. It does not say how many justices sit on it. That is left to Congress, which has changed the number repeatedly — six, seven, nine, ten — and settled on nine in 1869.

Lower federal courts are different. The text says Congress may create them, not that it must. For the first few years there were none. Congress created the first district and circuit courts in the Judiciary Act of 1789, and it could abolish them tomorrow.

That asymmetry is the single biggest check Congress has on the judiciary. It cannot fire judges, but it can shrink the courts and control what they are allowed to hear.

Section 1 — “during good Behaviour”

This is how federal judges get lifetime tenure without the phrase ever appearing. They serve until they die, resign, retire, or are impeached and convicted. There is no other way to remove a federal judge.

The salary rule closes an obvious loophole. Congress cannot cut a judge's pay to pressure a ruling. Inflation can erode it, and Congress can raise it, but it cannot be reduced.

Why insulate judges this way? Because courts are asked to make unpopular decisions. A judge who has to face voters after ruling against a popular law is a judge under pressure. Lifetime tenure is a bet that independence produces better law than accountability does.

It is a real bet, and it has costs. Federal judges can become incapacitated and stay in office. Misconduct is hard to punish. The counterargument is that the alternative — elected judges — creates its own problems, and several states run that experiment and can be compared directly.

Section 2 — What cases federal courts hear

Section 2 lists the categories of cases that may go to federal court. The two that matter most:

Cases “arising under this Constitution, the Laws of the United States, and Treaties.” This is the federal question power. Any case that turns on the meaning of the Constitution or federal law can be heard in federal court. Without this phrase, there would be no federal court system worth the name.

Controversies between citizens of different states. This is diversity jurisdiction. A resident of Ohio suing a company in Texas can go to federal court, on the theory that a local court might favor its own.

Section 2 also sets the difference between original and appellate jurisdiction. Original means the case starts there — mainly disputes involving ambassadors or a state as a party. Appellate means the court reviews what a lower court did. Almost everything the Supreme Court hears is appellate.

Note the phrase attached to appellate jurisdiction: with such Exceptions, and under such Regulations as the Congress shall make. Congress can strip cases out of the Supreme Court's appellate docket. It rarely has, because the political cost is high, but the power is written plainly.

Section 3 — Treason, defined narrowly

Treason is the only crime the Constitution defines. That was deliberate.

In England, treason law had been a political weapon for centuries. The charge could be stretched to cover criticizing the government, and conviction carried a horrifying death and the seizure of your family's property. The framers had read that history.

So they narrowed it hard. Treason requires an actual act — levying war or giving aid to an enemy — not an opinion, not a speech, not a plan. It requires either two witnesses to the same act or a confession in open court. And Congress may not punish a traitor's children by seizing the family's estate beyond the traitor's own lifetime.

Prosecutions for treason in the United States have been rare, and convictions rarer still. That is the clause working as intended.

The power that is not in the text

Here is the fact that reorganizes everything above. Nothing in Article III says the Supreme Court may strike down a law passed by Congress.

The power is called judicial review, and the Court gave it to itself in 1803, in Marbury v. Madison.

The case began with a bureaucratic mess. In the final hours of John Adams's presidency, Adams appointed a number of judges, including William Marbury as a justice of the peace in the District of Columbia. The Senate confirmed Marbury. His signed commission was sealed and ready. Then it was never delivered.

Thomas Jefferson took office and told his secretary of state, James Madison, not to deliver it. Marbury sued, asking the Supreme Court to order Madison to hand over the paper.

Chief Justice John Marshall's opinion did three things in sequence. First, he said Marbury was legally entitled to the commission. Second, he said the law gave him a remedy. Third, he said the Court could not provide it — because the section of the Judiciary Act that let Marbury file directly in the Supreme Court was itself unconstitutional.

Congress had given the Court more original jurisdiction than Article III allowed, and Congress cannot do that. So the Court struck down part of a federal statute. It was the first time it had ever done so.

The move was politically brilliant. Jefferson got the outcome he wanted — Marbury never became a justice of the peace. And the Court, in the act of giving him that outcome, claimed a power far larger than the case.

Marshall's reasoning ran like this. The Constitution is supreme law. A law that conflicts with it cannot be valid. Somebody has to decide whether a conflict exists. In a case properly before them, that somebody is the courts.

Jefferson never accepted it. He argued that each branch interprets the Constitution for itself, and that a court's ruling binds the parties in that case but not the other branches. That position has never won. Judicial review was used sparingly for decades — the second time the Court struck down a federal law was Dred Scott in 1857 — but it was never seriously reversed.

Why It Matters

Article III created a court system almost by accident, and then that system became the final word on what the Constitution means. Nearly every page in the rest of this project depends on that fact. When we say the Fourteenth Amendment “now applies to the states,” we mean the Supreme Court said so.

That is an enormous amount of authority to rest on a power the text does not mention. It is also, arguably, unavoidable. A written constitution that nothing can enforce is just a pamphlet. Someone has to hold the document above the ordinary laws, and the courts are the institution the framers built for the job.

The practical result is that the appointment of judges is a constitutional event, not an administrative one. Every confirmation fight is a fight about Article III — about who gets to say what the document means for the next thirty years.
