Part Four · The Amendments Since 1791

Amendment 20 — Lame Ducks

Ratified 1933 Six sections of plumbing 4 min read

In the Text

Plain English

This amendment contains no great principle. It fixes a timetable, and the timetable had nearly wrecked the country.

The old calendar

Under the original Constitution, a president elected in November did not take office until March 4 of the following year. That is four months of a defeated president still running the government.

Congress had it worse. New members elected in November did not take their seats until December of the next year — thirteen months later. A Congress that voters had thrown out could keep legislating for a full session after the election.

These departing officials were called lame ducks. They had no reason to care what voters thought, because voters could not touch them again.

Why 1933 forced the issue

Herbert Hoover lost the 1932 election to Franklin Roosevelt in a landslide. The country was four years into the Depression, and banks were collapsing.

Between November 1932 and March 1933, Hoover was still president and Roosevelt had no power. Hoover wanted Roosevelt to commit to his approach before cooperating; Roosevelt wanted to wait. The result was four months of paralysis while the banking system failed.

By inauguration day, most banks in the country had closed or were about to. The amendment had been proposed in 1932, before any of this. It was ratified in January 1933, and the crisis made the case for it obvious.

The new rules

Presidential terms now begin January 20. Congressional terms begin January 3, and Congress must meet at least once a year starting then.

The rest of the amendment handles contingencies. If the president-elect dies before taking office, the vice president-elect becomes president. If no president has qualified by inauguration day, Congress decides who acts as president until one does.

It also moved up the effective date for Sections 1 and 2 to October 1933, for reasons of pure logistics.

Why It Matters

This is the least glamorous amendment in the document and one of the most useful. It solved a genuine defect in the original design — one the framers could not have anticipated, because they did not expect a national crisis to demand immediate action.

It also illustrates something about constitutional repair. The framers were careful about who holds power and careless about how quickly power transfers. They thought about usurpation and not about a bank run.

There is a modern echo worth noticing. The transition period is now ten weeks instead of four months, and people still argue it is too long. When a government changes hands, the gap between the decision and the handover is itself a constitutional question.
