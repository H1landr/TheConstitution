Part Four · The Amendments Since 1791

Amendment 24 — Poll Taxes

Ratified 1964 94 years after the Fifteenth 4 min read

In the Text

Plain English

A poll tax is a fee charged for the privilege of voting. This amendment bans them, but only in elections for federal office.

Why poll taxes existed

After the Fifteenth Amendment barred racial discrimination in voting, southern states needed methods that were formally race-neutral. A fee to vote was perfect on paper. It applied to everyone.

In practice it applied to almost nobody white. Poor white voters were often exempted by grandfather clauses, and in many places the fee was simply not collected from white voters. For Black voters, it was enforced.

The amounts were not trivial. A few dollars in the 1890s was a meaningful sum for a sharecropper. In some states the tax had to be paid for several consecutive years before you could vote at all.

The long delay

The Fifteenth Amendment was ratified in 1870. The Twenty-Fourth was ratified in 1964. That is ninety-four years.

Congress proposed the amendment in 1962. Ratification took eighteen months, which by the standards of this document is fast. Illinois was the 38th state, in January 1964.

What it does not cover

Read the sentence again: elections for president, vice president, senators, and representatives. Federal elections only.

State and local elections were left out. Two years later, in 1966, the Supreme Court closed that gap on different grounds. In Harper v. Virginia Board of Elections, the Court held that a poll tax in a state election violates the equal protection clause. Its reasoning was that wealth is not a legitimate basis for deciding who may vote.

So the Twenty-Fourth Amendment was, in a sense, overtaken. It banned poll taxes in federal elections by amendment, and the courts banned them everywhere else under a different provision.

Why It Matters

This amendment is one of the clearest illustrations of how long a constitutional promise can take to become real. The Fifteenth Amendment said race could not block the vote in 1870. Poll taxes blocked it anyway for nearly a century, because the amendment did not anticipate a fee.

It also shows the difference between the two routes to constitutional change. The Twenty-Fourth took two years of amendment politics and covered one kind of election. Harper took a single court decision and covered all of them. When the courts can act, they act faster.

There is a cost to that speed, and the country has argued about it ever since. Harper rests on the idea that wealth cannot be a voting qualification. That principle now governs a wide range of election rules — and how far it extends, to ID requirements or to fines for unpaid fees, is still being litigated.
