Part Four · The Amendments Since 1791

Amendment 12 — Electing a President

Ratified 1804 A repair job 5 min read

In the Text

Plain English

Nine words: distinct ballots. That is the entire amendment, and it exists because the original system nearly broke the country in 1800.

The original design

Article II asked each elector to vote for two people. There was no separate vote for president and vice president. Whoever got the most votes became president, and whoever came second became vice president.

The framers assumed this would produce sensible results. They did not expect political parties.

Parties appeared almost immediately. In 1796, John Adams won the presidency and Thomas Jefferson — his opponent — became his vice president. The two men were rivals running against each other, and now one served under the other.

1800

In 1800, Jefferson ran for president with Aaron Burr as his running mate. The plan was that one elector would withhold a vote so Jefferson would finish first and Burr second.

The plan failed. Jefferson and Burr each received 73 electoral votes — a tie.

Under the Constitution, a tie went to the House of Representatives. The House was controlled by the outgoing Federalists, who disliked Jefferson intensely. They deadlocked and voted 35 times without a result.

Alexander Hamilton, who disliked Burr far more than he disliked Jefferson, lobbied Federalist members to give in. On the 36th ballot, Jefferson won.

Burr became vice president anyway, and then killed Hamilton in a duel three years later. American politics in the early republic was not gentle.

The fix

The Twelfth Amendment requires electors to cast separate votes for the two offices. It also reduced the House's choices from the top five to the top three when it has to break a tie.

One section of the amendment, printed in brackets in many texts, dealt with what happens if the House fails to choose a president by March 4. The Twentieth Amendment later changed that date and made the language obsolete.

Why It Matters

The Twelfth Amendment is unusual because it does not add a right or restrict a power. It fixes an engineering problem. The designers built a machine that assumed no political parties would exist, and the first generation of politicians proved that assumption wrong.

It also did not solve everything. The House has chosen the president twice — in 1800 and in 1824 — and the possibility never went away. If no candidate reaches 270 electoral votes, the election goes to the House under rules where each state delegation gets one vote.

That has come close in modern times. In 1968, a third-party candidate nearly threw the election into the House. Every serious third-party campaign since has carried the same possibility. The system is one regional candidate away from a constitutional crisis, and the amendment that was supposed to fix things left that door open.
