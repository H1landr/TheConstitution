Part Two · The Seven Articles

Article IV — The States

Four sections The glue between the states 7 min read

In the Text

Plain English

Section 1 — Full faith and credit

The United States is fifty separate legal systems. Article IV is the reason that barely matters in daily life.

Section 1 says every state must honor the official acts of every other state. A marriage, a will, a birth certificate, a court judgment, a driver's license — all of it travels. Without this clause, a divorce granted in Nevada would mean nothing in Ohio, and a contract enforced in one state could be ignored next door.

Congress fills in the details by statute. The practical effect is that a judgment you win in one state is generally enforceable in the others.

There is one real limit: states are not required to enforce another state's criminal punishments. A state cannot ship someone home to serve a sentence by invoking this clause. And the clause is about recognizing acts, not about adopting another state's policy. When states disagree sharply on a social question, that distinction does a lot of work — and it is exactly where the hardest arguments have happened.

Section 2 — Privileges and immunities

Citizens of each state get the privileges and immunities of citizens in the other states. In plain terms: a state cannot treat you worse because you live somewhere else.

It is not unlimited. Courts read it to cover fundamental things — the right to work, own property, and use the courts. It does not require identical treatment in everything. State universities can charge out-of-state tuition. Hunting licenses can cost more for visitors. Those are considered reasonable distinctions rather than discrimination.

One trap worth flagging now, because it will trip you up later. This is not the same as the privileges or immunities clause in the Fourteenth Amendment. There are two clauses with nearly the same name, in two different articles, doing two different jobs. This one is about how states treat outsiders. The Fourteenth Amendment one is about how states treat their own citizens.

Section 2 — Extradition

If someone charged with a serious crime flees to another state, the governor of the state that wants them must, on request, hand them over. This is extradition.

For over a century it was unenforceable. An 1861 Supreme Court decision held that governors could not be compelled to comply, and some simply refused. The Court reversed that in 1987, and the duty is now enforceable in court. Governors still occasionally refuse on policy grounds, but they no longer have the legal cover they once did.

Section 2 — The fugitive slave clause

That paragraph is the third one in Section 2, and it is the ugliest thing in the original Constitution. It requires free states to return people who had escaped slavery.

Notice that the word slave does not appear. Neither does slavery. The delegates chose “Person held to Service or Labour” — language that could describe an indentured servant, which it was designed to blur. This was a deliberate choice by men who knew exactly what they were writing.

It was enforced. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 created federal commissioners to seize people in free states, and it required ordinary citizens to help. Free Black Americans who had lived in the North for decades were dragged south on the testimony of a claimant, with no jury and no right to testify.

The Thirteenth Amendment ended it. Ratified in 1865, it abolished slavery and involuntary servitude outright, and a clause requiring the return of enslaved people cannot survive that. The words are still printed in Article IV. They are dead letter, and they stay in the document because the Constitution is amended, not rewritten.

Section 3 — New states and territories

Congress admits new states. It cannot carve a state out of an existing one without that state's permission, and it cannot merge two states without both.

Thirty-seven states joined under this section. Maine came from Massachusetts with consent. West Virginia split from Virginia in 1863 under circumstances the Supreme Court never fully settled. Vermont resolved a long-running dispute with New York.

The second paragraph gives Congress power over territories — land belonging to the United States but not yet a state. That clause is why Congress could govern the western territories, and why it still governs Puerto Rico, Guam, and the U.S. Virgin Islands, none of which are states.

Section 4 — The Guarantee Clause

The United States guarantees every state a Republican Form of Government, and promises to protect each state against invasion and, when asked, against domestic violence.

The phrase is never defined, and courts have largely refused to define it. Challenges under the Guarantee Clause are usually dismissed as political questions — disputes the judiciary leaves to the elected branches. So a promise sits in the text with no clear mechanism to enforce it.

The protection half has been used far more. It is one legal basis for sending federal troops when a state cannot keep order — including during desegregation in the 1950s and 1960s.

Why It Matters

Article IV is the least famous article and the one you rely on most often without noticing. A bank accepts an out-of-state check. A college reads a transcript from another state. A court enforces a judgment from across the country. This article is doing the work in all three cases.

It also shows the Constitution's two faces side by side. Section 1 is practical machinery that makes a continent function as one economy and one legal space. Two sections later, the same article orders the return of escaped human beings.

That pairing is not an accident of drafting. It is what the 1787 bargain actually looked like when written down. Reading Article IV straight through is one of the fastest ways to understand both what the document accomplished and what it cost.
