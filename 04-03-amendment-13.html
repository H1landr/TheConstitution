Part Four · The Amendments Since 1791

Amendment 13 — Slavery Abolished

Ratified 1865 The hinge of the document 5 min read

In the Text

Plain English

Twenty-nine words that ended an institution the original Constitution had protected. Everything after this amendment in American history is different because of it.

Notice what the sentence does not do. It does not say who is free. It does not pay anyone. It does not mention race. It abolishes a legal status, everywhere, permanently.

It reaches beyond the federal government and beyond the states that had slavery. It covers “any place subject to their jurisdiction,” which includes territories and, later, overseas possessions.

Congress proposed it on January 31, 1865, while the Civil War was still running. It was ratified on December 6 of that year. Section 2 gives Congress the power to enforce it by legislation, which is the constitutional basis for the Civil Rights Act of 1866.

The exception

Except as a punishment for crime. Those seven words have a history of their own, and it is not a good one.

After the war, southern states passed Black Codes that criminalized vagrancy, loitering, and failing to hold a job. Convicts were then leased out to plantations, mines, and railroads. The practice was called convict leasing, and it lasted into the twentieth century. People were, in practical terms, re-enslaved through the criminal system.

The exception is still real law. Courts have consistently held that prison labor is permitted because of it. Where the line sits — whether a state can require unpaid labor from someone on probation, for instance — is contested and has been litigated in recent years.

What else counts as involuntary servitude

Courts have held that the phrase does not cover every obligation. The military draft was upheld during the First World War. Jury duty, taxes, and required civic service are not servitude.

What it clearly does cover is coercive labor arrangements outside the criminal system — debt bondage, peonage, and trafficking. Those prosecutions rest on this amendment.

Why It Matters

The original Constitution never used the words slave or slavery. It referred to “persons held to Service or Labour” and counted enslaved people as three-fifths of a person for representation. The document was built around the institution without naming it.

The Thirteenth Amendment names it and kills it. That is why the Civil War is often described as a second founding. The constitutional order before 1865 and the one after are not the same country.

But the amendment also shows how an exception can outlive its purpose. The framers of the Thirteenth Amendment were not trying to create a loophole for forced labor. They were acknowledging that imprisonment involves coercion. The loophole was built afterward, by state legislatures, using the words the amendment left available.

That pattern repeats across the document. A clause is written with one case in mind, and later generations find uses the authors never imagined. Sometimes those uses expand freedom. Sometimes they do the opposite.
