A guide for students

The Constitution,
Explained

The full document, taken apart one piece at a time. Every page shows you the actual words, says what they mean in plain English, and explains why the people who wrote them thought it mattered.

What this is

The Constitution of the United States is about 4,500 words long. It is the oldest written national constitution still in use, and almost nobody reads it. That is a shame, because it is not actually hard. It is just old, and it is written in the flat, careful language of lawyers who were afraid of being misunderstood.

This guide walks through the whole thing — the preamble, the seven articles, and all twenty-seven amendments — in order. Each piece gets the same three-part treatment, so you always know what you are looking at.

One: the text

First you get the words. Not a summary — the actual sentence, spelled and capitalized the way it appears on the parchment, with a citation so you can look it up yourself. It is quoted in a block with a red rule down the side.

Two: plain English

Then the same thing, said normally. This is where most of the work happens. Legal writing hides its meaning inside long sentences and words borrowed from French and Latin. Untangled, most of it turns out to be surprisingly direct.

Three: why it matters

Last, the reason the clause exists. Almost every line in the Constitution is a response to something that had already gone wrong, or something the authors were afraid would go wrong later. Knowing which one is usually the difference between remembering a rule and understanding it.

How to read it

Straight through, if you like — it is written in order and each page assumes you have read the ones before it. Or start with whatever you came for. If you want the parts people argue about most, go to the Second Amendment, the Fourteenth, or the powers of the president.

A note on honesty: this guide is careful to keep three things apart — what the text says, what courts have decided it means, and what actually happens in practice. Those three are often different. When a question is genuinely disputed, this guide says so instead of picking a side.

Nothing here is legal advice. It is a reading guide. If you have a real legal problem, talk to a lawyer.
