Part Four · The Amendments Since 1791

Amendment 21 — Repeal

Ratified 1933 Two records at once 4 min read

In the Text

Plain English

Eighteen words, and the only time the Constitution has taken something back.

Two things it does that nothing else does

First, it repeals another amendment. No other amendment has done that.

Second, it was ratified by state conventions rather than by state legislatures. Article V allows either method, and Congress chooses. This is the only time the convention method has been used.

Why the conventions

Because the state legislatures were the problem.

Prohibition had been ratified by legislatures, and in many states the legislature was dominated by rural districts where the temperance movement was strong. Even as public opinion turned against Prohibition, those legislatures stayed dry.

Conventions are elected directly by voters for the specific purpose of deciding the question. Congress chose that route deliberately, and it worked. The amendment was ratified in ten months.

Section 2, which is still alive

The second section prohibits transporting or importing liquor into any state in violation of that state's laws. It sounds like leftover housekeeping. It is not.

This clause is the constitutional foundation for state control of alcohol, and the United States still has a patchwork of state liquor laws unlike anything else in the developed world. Some states run liquor stores. Some allow sales only in private shops. Some control which beers and wines can be sold, and through what distribution chain.

Courts have read Section 2 as giving states unusual authority over alcohol, even where it would otherwise conflict with the Constitution's commerce clause. That is why a state can regulate alcohol in ways it could not regulate televisions or books.

Why It Matters

The Twenty-First Amendment proves two things at once.

The first is that a constitutional amendment is not permanent. A supermajority put Prohibition in, and a supermajority took it out. Thirteen years apart.

The second is that the amendment process is flexible in ways people forget. Article V offers four routes. Only two have been used to ratify, but the convention route was not merely available — it was chosen precisely because it solved a political problem the legislature route could not.

There is a broader lesson buried in the mechanics. The framers built a system that is hard to change, and they were right that difficulty prevents rash change. But when an amendment turns out to be a mistake, the same difficulty applies to fixing it. Prohibition had to fail visibly, for over a decade, before the country could reverse course.
