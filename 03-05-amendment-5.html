Part Three · The Bill of Rights

Amendment 5 — Due Process

Ratified 1791 Five protections 6 min read

In the Text

Plain English

One long sentence containing five separate protections. Four are about criminal cases. The fifth is about property, and it is the one that reaches almost everyone.

1. Grand jury indictment

For serious federal crimes, a prosecutor cannot simply charge you. A grand jury — a group of citizens, usually 16 to 23, meeting in secret — must first agree there is enough evidence.

It is a one-sided proceeding. The defense is not in the room, and the prosecutor presents the case. The saying among lawyers is that a grand jury would indict a ham sandwich. That is the point and the criticism at once.

Grand juries are also not required by the states. This is one of the few parts of the Bill of Rights that was never applied to state governments, so many state prosecutions begin with a different procedure.

2. Double jeopardy

If you are acquitted, the government cannot try you again for the same offense. If you are convicted and punished, it cannot try you again either.

There is a large exception. Separate governments can each prosecute you. A state and the federal government are treated as separate sovereigns, so a federal acquittal does not bar a state trial, and the reverse is also true. After the 1992 acquittals of officers in the Rodney King case, the federal government charged them with civil rights violations and obtained convictions. That was not double jeopardy under this rule.

3. Self-incrimination

You cannot be forced to testify against yourself in a criminal case. This is the right people mean when they say they are taking the Fifth.

It protects testimony, not physical evidence. You can be required to give fingerprints, a blood sample, or a handwriting sample. You cannot be required to say what you did.

The protection applies outside the courtroom too. In 1966, in Miranda v. Arizona, the Supreme Court held that police must inform a suspect of the right before questioning. Those warnings exist because otherwise the right would be useless in the room where it matters most.

One thing this right does not do: it cannot be used to protect someone else. You cannot refuse to answer because your answer would incriminate a friend.

4. Due process

The government cannot take your life, liberty, or property without fair legal procedure. That is the procedural meaning, and it includes notice, a hearing, and an impartial decision-maker.

Courts have also read a substantive meaning into the phrase — that some rights are so fundamental that no amount of fair procedure justifies taking them. That reading is powerful and controversial, and it is the basis for many of the most contested decisions in American law.

Notice that the same phrase appears in the Fourteenth Amendment, aimed at the states. This one limits the federal government. The other one limits everyone else.

5. Taking property

Known as the takings clause. If the government takes your property for a public use, it has to pay a fair price.

The hard questions are what counts as a taking and what counts as public use. A regulation that makes your land nearly worthless may count as a taking even though the government never takes title. And in 2005, in Kelo v. City of New London, the Court held that economic development could count as a public use, allowing a city to take homes for a private development project. The backlash was severe. Most states changed their laws in response.

Why It Matters

The Fifth Amendment is the procedural conscience of the document. It says that how the government acts matters, not just what it achieves. Prosecutors must convince a grand jury. Jurors must be told the state carries the burden. Property owners must be paid.

Its most quoted clause is also its most elastic. “Due process of law” has become one of the largest ideas in American constitutionalism, and it grew far beyond anything a reader in 1791 would have recognized. Whether that growth was legitimate is one of the permanent arguments.
