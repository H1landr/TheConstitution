Part Zero · Before the Document

The Articles of Confederation

In force 1781–1789 5 min read

In the Text

Plain English

A firm league of friendship. That phrase is the whole design, and it explains almost everything that went wrong.

The Articles did not create a government over the American people. They created an alliance among thirteen sovereign states, each keeping its own army, its own money, and its own trade policy. The states promised to help one another, roughly the way allies do.

What it set up

One house of Congress. One vote per state, no matter how large. No president. No national court system.

Congress could declare war, make treaties, run a post office, borrow money, and ask the states for troops. That is close to the full list. It is an adequate set of powers for a wartime alliance and a hopeless set for a functioning country.

What it could not do

It could not tax. Congress could add up what it needed and send each state a bill. States paid what they felt like paying, which was usually very little or nothing at all. The national government borrowed heavily during the Revolution and then had no reliable way to repay it.

It could not regulate trade between the states. States taxed goods coming from their neighbors and printed their own paper money. New York and New Jersey spent years fighting over shipping fees, and the quarrel turned genuinely nasty. There was no court that could settle it.

It could not enforce anything. Congress passed laws and then depended entirely on the states to carry them out. There was no executive branch to enforce them and no court to order anyone to comply.

It could not easily be fixed. Major laws needed the votes of nine of the thirteen states. Amending the Articles needed all thirteen. So any single state could block any change, forever.

The rebellion that broke it

In 1786, farmers in western Massachusetts who could not pay their debts faced losing their land. Led in part by a Revolutionary War veteran named Daniel Shays, they shut down courthouses to stop the foreclosures. A privately funded militia eventually broke the uprising.

The national government played almost no role, because it had no money and no troops. That failure landed hard. If a rebellion could not be put down without a private army, the league of friendship was not providing friendship, and it was not providing defence.

Why It Matters

The Constitution is easiest to understand as a list of repairs. Once you know what broke, the new document stops looking arbitrary and starts looking like a response.

Congress gets an explicit power to tax, to borrow, and to regulate commerce — three direct answers to the three failures above. Article II creates a president, partly so that somebody is responsible for carrying out the laws. Article III creates courts, partly so that states have somewhere to sue each other that is not a border dispute.

And the voting rules changed in a way that is easy to miss. The new Constitution took effect when nine states ratified it, not all thirteen. The framers had just watched unanimity paralyze the country, and they refused to build that trap a second time.

One thing did not change, and it is worth naming. The Articles left the question of slavery almost entirely to the states. The Constitution did the same, in more careful language. That silence was a choice, and it carried a price the country would pay for another eighty years.
