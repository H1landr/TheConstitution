Part Four · The Amendments Since 1791

Amendment 19 — Women's Suffrage

Ratified 1920 72 years of campaigning 5 min read

In the Text

Plain English

The structure is identical to the Fifteenth Amendment, with one word changed: sex instead of race.

It is again a prohibition, not a grant. States could still set their own voting qualifications. They just could not use sex as one of them.

Before 1920

The campaign officially began in 1848 at Seneca Falls, New York, where a convention produced a declaration modeled on the Declaration of Independence. It took 72 years to reach ratification.

It was not the first time women voted in America. New Jersey's first constitution let property-owning women vote until the legislature revoked it in 1807. Wyoming's territorial government granted women the vote in 1869, and several western states followed. By 1919, fifteen states already allowed it.

The western states mattered strategically. Suffragists pointed out that women were already voting in those places and the sky had not fallen, which made the argument harder to dismiss.

The last vote

Congress proposed the amendment in June 1919. Ratification needed 36 states, and by the summer of 1920 it stood at 35.

Tennessee was the deciding vote. The measure appeared to fail in the state house, then passed by a single vote when a 24-year-old member named Harry Burn changed his position.

He had received a note from his mother. It read, in part: “Hurrah, and vote for suffrage. Don't keep them in doubt.” Burn later said he had not been sure before reading it. The Nineteenth Amendment was ratified on August 18, 1920.

Who was still left out

The amendment ended sex discrimination in voting. It did not end the other forms.

Black women in the South faced the same literacy tests, poll taxes, and intimidation that Black men did. Many were effectively barred until 1965.

Native American women were not U.S. citizens in 1920. Citizenship was granted to Native Americans generally in 1924, and some states blocked them from voting for decades after that.

Asian immigrants were barred from citizenship until the 1940s and 1950s, and therefore from voting.

Why It Matters

This amendment roughly doubled the size of the electorate. It is the largest single expansion of American democracy by any means, and it happened through the hardest available process.

Its passage is also a case study in how amendment campaigns actually work. The suffragists ran a state-by-state strategy for decades, winning referenda and court cases one at a time. They built a patchwork of state laws that made the national amendment look like the obvious next step rather than a radical change.

And the Tennessee story is worth keeping. One vote, one note from a mother, one state. A constitutional amendment of enormous consequence turned on a decision made that morning.
