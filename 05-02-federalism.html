Part Five · Putting It Together

Federalism: Who Does What

Two governments, one country 6 min read

In the Text

Plain English

The United States does not have one government. It has thousands, layered on top of each other, and the Constitution only settles which layer does what at the edges.

You are governed by a town, a county, a state, and a federal government at the same time. Each can tax you, each can arrest you, and each has its own courts. That is unusual. Most countries run a single national legal system with local offices that answer to it.

The rough division

Mostly federal: currency, war and defense, borders, immigration, treaties, the postal service, patents, and interstate commerce.

Mostly state: criminal law, family law, marriage and divorce, property, contracts, education, licensing, and the rules for running elections.

Both, heavily: taxation, courts, health care, transportation, environmental regulation, and benefits programs.

That third category is enormous, and it is where nearly all the conflict lives.

How the line has moved

In 1789 the federal government was tiny. It collected customs duties, ran a post office, and paid down war debt. Almost everything else happened at the state level.

Four things moved the line.

The Civil War and the Reconstruction amendments. The Fourteenth Amendment gave the federal government a permanent role in policing how states treat people.

The Sixteenth Amendment. An income tax gave Congress money at a scale it had never had.

The New Deal. A broad reading of the commerce clause let Congress regulate things that had always been state business, on the theory that they affected interstate trade.

Grants-in-aid. Congress raises money nationally and offers it to states with conditions attached. A state that wants the highway money accepts the drinking age of 21. That is not a command. It is a deal the state is free to refuse, and almost never does.

Where the line is being pushed back

Since the 1990s, the Supreme Court has placed some limits on federal reach. It struck down a federal law banning guns near schools, holding that the connection to interstate commerce was too thin. It limited Congress's ability to commandeer state officials to carry out federal programs. It narrowed one part of the Affordable Care Act's Medicaid expansion, on the ground that the financial pressure crossed from inducement into coercion.

These are real limits, but they are narrow. The overall direction of the last century has been toward more federal authority, not less.

The honest summary

There is no formula in the Constitution that draws a clean line. The Tenth Amendment says powers not delegated stay with the states, but it does not say which powers those are. As one 1985 decision put it, the amendment states a truism without telling courts how to apply it.

So the line gets drawn case by case, by courts and by Congress, and it moves.

Why It Matters

Most American political arguments are federalism arguments wearing a costume.

Should abortion be legal? That is partly a question about rights and partly a question about which government decides. Same for guns, schools, health insurance, minimum wage, and environmental rules. Strip the policy away and what remains is: who should be making this decision?

Federalism also has genuine costs and genuine benefits, and both are real. Fifty states can experiment, and successful experiments spread. Fifty states can also produce fifty different answers to a question that does not respect state lines, and can leave people's rights depending on where they happen to live.

The Constitution does not resolve that trade-off. It provides a structure in which the country can keep arguing about it.
