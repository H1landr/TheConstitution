Part Three · The Bill of Rights

Amendment 9 — Unlisted Rights

Ratified 1791 One sentence 4 min read

In the Text

Plain English

This amendment exists to answer a specific objection: if you write down a list of rights, people will assume the list is complete.

The objection it answers

When Congress debated a bill of rights in 1789, the Federalists made an argument against having one at all. If the Constitution enumerates the rights the government must respect, a lawyer will eventually say: everything not on the list is fair game.

Alexander Hamilton made this case in Federalist No. 84. He called a bill of rights dangerous, for precisely that reason.

The Ninth Amendment is the response. It says: no, the list does not work that way. The fact that a right is written down does not mean it is the only right you have.

Enumerate means to list one by one. So the sentence reads: listing some rights does not cancel the others.

What it has actually done

Very little, on its own. The Ninth Amendment has almost never been the basis for a Supreme Court decision. It appears in concurring opinions more than in holdings.

Justice Arthur Goldberg relied on it in 1965, in a concurrence arguing that the Constitution protects a right of marital privacy even though no clause says so. That reasoning influenced later privacy cases, but the majority opinions in those cases rested on due process instead.

The clause is hard to use because it does not say which unlisted rights exist. It says they exist. It does not name them, rank them, or explain how to identify one.

Where the unlisted rights went

They went into the due process clauses instead. Courts protect rights the text does not name — to marry, to have children, to direct your own medical care, to raise your children as you see fit. They do it by calling these liberties protected by due process.

So the Ninth Amendment lost the argument about method while winning the argument about substance. Unenumerated rights are real. They are just housed in a different clause than the one written for them.

Why It Matters

The Ninth Amendment is the Constitution admitting that it is incomplete. That is a remarkable thing for a founding document to concede.

It matters most in arguments about how to read the document. If you believe courts should protect only the rights actually written down, the Ninth Amendment is a problem for you, because it says plainly that other rights exist. If you believe courts may protect unwritten rights, the amendment supports you but gives you no method for deciding which ones.

Either way, the sentence forecloses one reading: that the Bill of Rights is a complete inventory of American liberty. The framers thought about that objection and answered it directly. They just did not tell us what to do next.
