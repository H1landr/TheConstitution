Part Three · The Bill of Rights

Amendment 1 — Five Freedoms

Ratified 1791 45 words 6 min read

In the Text

Plain English

Five freedoms in one sentence, and not one of them is absolute. Every phrase here has been argued over for two centuries.

Start with the opening. Congress shall make no law. Read literally, that limits Congress and nobody else. For the first 130 years, that is exactly how it worked — a state could censor a newspaper and the First Amendment had nothing to say. That changed in the twentieth century. See the page on incorporation for how.

Religion, in two directions

The amendment has an establishment clause and a free exercise clause. The first says government cannot set up or favor an official religion. The second says government cannot stop you from practicing yours.

Those two can pull against each other. A law exempting a religious group from a general rule looks like free exercise to one person and like establishment to another.

How courts decide establishment cases has changed significantly. From 1971 to 2022, most cases used the Lemon test, which asked whether a law had a secular purpose and whether it entangled government with religion. In 2022 the Supreme Court set that test aside in favor of an approach based on historical practice. This is live, moving law, and any summary of it goes stale quickly.

Free exercise draws a line between belief and action. Your belief is absolutely protected. Your conduct is not. The Supreme Court upheld a polygamy conviction in 1878 and held in 1990 that a neutral, generally applicable law can apply even when it burdens a religious practice. Congress responded in 1993 with the Religious Freedom Restoration Act, which gives religious claims more protection at the federal level.

Speech

The core rule is that government generally cannot punish you for what you say because it dislikes the content. That includes speech most people find offensive, which is the whole point.

But the protection is not unlimited, and the categories are narrow. Speech can be regulated when it is intended and likely to produce imminent lawless action. True threats and harassment are unprotected. So is obscenity, defined by a specific test rather than by taste. So is fraud, and so is defamation of a private person. Fighting words, spoken face to face and likely to provoke a fight, are also outside.

Government can also impose neutral rules on the time, place, and manner of speech. A city can require a permit to march. It cannot require a permit only for marches it disagrees with.

Press

The press clause is mostly about one thing: stopping government from blocking publication in advance. That practice is called prior restraint, and courts treat it as close to a constitutional last resort.

The government lost that fight in 1971, when it tried to stop the New York Times from publishing the Pentagon Papers. It also lost in 1931, when Minnesota tried to shut down a scandal sheet through a nuisance law.

This does not mean reporters are immune from everything. They can be subpoenaed and can be required to testify, and most states protect them through statutes rather than through the Constitution.

Assembly and petition

You have the right to gather peacefully and to ask the government for change. That second one sounds like a minor formality. It is actually the oldest right in the list, and the one most directly aimed at the grievance that started the Revolution.

Assemblies can be regulated, but the rules have to be neutral and cannot be used to single out an unpopular group. Courts watch protest permit schemes closely for that reason.

Why It Matters

The First Amendment is the most litigated provision in the Constitution, and the one whose meaning has changed the most. It is also the place where the American system is most distinct. Most countries protect speech. This one protects speech that is widely regarded as worthless, on the theory that the alternative is worse.

The disagreements are real and durable. Should money count as speech? Should a website designer be required to create content for a wedding she opposes? Should a school be able to lead a prayer? All three have reached the Supreme Court, and none is settled for good.

What the amendment does establish is a starting presumption: when the government tries to restrict expression, the burden falls on the government to justify it. That presumption is worth more than any particular outcome.
