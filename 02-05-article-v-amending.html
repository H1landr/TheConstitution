Part Two · The Seven Articles

Article V — Amending

One sentence 27 amendments in 235 years 6 min read

In the Text

Plain English

That is the entire amendment process, and it is one sentence. It is also the only place in the document that explains how to change the document.

The sentence contains four possible paths, because it offers two ways to propose an amendment and two ways to ratify one. Combine them and you get four routes.

Four routes, two used

To propose: either two-thirds of both houses of Congress vote for it, or two-thirds of the state legislatures ask Congress to call a convention.

To ratify: either three-fourths of the state legislatures approve, or three-fourths of state ratifying conventions approve. Congress chooses which.

In practice, only two of the four have ever been used. Every amendment except one was proposed by Congress and ratified by state legislatures. The exception is the Twenty-First Amendment, which repealed Prohibition and was ratified by state conventions instead.

The convention route — the one where the states call a convention — has never been used, not once. Two-thirds of state legislatures have come close on a balanced budget amendment at various points, but never crossed the line. Part of the reason is that nobody knows the rules. How are delegates chosen? Can a convention be limited to one topic? What happens if it proposes something else? Article V is silent, so the route stays theoretical.

The two locks

Article V also contains two provisions that cannot be amended the normal way.

The first protected the slave trade from being banned before 1808. It expired on its own and is now dead text.

The second is permanent. No state can be deprived of its equal vote in the Senate without its own consent. That means the Senate's structure cannot be changed by amendment. Wyoming's two seats cannot be taken away by three-fourths of the states, because Wyoming would have to agree — and it never would. The only way to alter the Senate is for every state to consent.

Why it is this hard

Twenty-seven amendments in two hundred and thirty-five years. Ten of those came in one batch in 1791, and three more in the five years after the Civil War. Long stretches pass with none at all — the sixty-one years between 1804 and 1865 produced not a single amendment.

The arithmetic explains it. Three-fourths of the states means 38 of 50. Thirteen states can block anything, and thirteen states is a small enough group to assemble. There is no way to gerrymander around it, and no way to wait out a deadline that does not exist.

Compare the states. State constitutions are amended constantly, sometimes dozens of times per decade, because most require only a simple majority vote of the people. Alabama's constitution has been amended more than 900 times. The federal document has been amended 27.

What gets through, and what does not

The amendments that succeed tend to share one feature: they fix something almost everyone has already agreed is broken.

Several amendments were passed specifically to overturn Supreme Court decisions. The Eleventh reversed a 1793 ruling that let citizens sue states. The Fourteenth reversed Dred Scott. The Sixteenth reversed a decision striking down the income tax. The Twenty-Sixth reversed a ruling that let Congress set a minimum voting age for federal elections only.

The ones that fail are the ones that would settle a live political fight. The Equal Rights Amendment passed Congress in 1972 with a seven-year deadline. It fell three states short. Virginia became the 38th state to ratify in 2020, long after the deadline, and whether that counts is still disputed.

Some amendments have been pending for over two centuries. The Corwin Amendment, which would have protected slavery, was passed by Congress in 1861 and never ratified. It is technically still open.

Why It Matters

Article V makes a trade. It buys stability at the price of responsiveness. A constitution that changed as easily as a statute would be no constitution at all — it would just be the current majority's preferences written in a fancy font.

But the trade has a cost that is easy to miss. When formal amendment is nearly impossible, change does not stop. It moves to the other channels: court interpretation, executive practice, and congressional statute. Those channels are faster, but they are also less legitimate in a specific way. Nobody voted for a Supreme Court ruling.

So the real function of Article V is not just to protect the document. It determines who gets to change it. Make amendment harder and you make judges more powerful, because they become the only actors who can respond to a changed country. Every argument about amending the Constitution is partly an argument about that.
