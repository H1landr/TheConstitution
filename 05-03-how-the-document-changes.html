Part Five · Putting It Together

How the Document Changes

Three channels 6 min read

In the Text

Plain English

The Constitution you live under is not the 1789 text. It is the 1789 text plus two hundred and thirty-five years of change, and only a fraction of that change came through Article V.

Channel one: amendment

This is the formal route, and it is the one everybody knows about. Two-thirds of Congress proposes, three-fourths of the states ratify. It has produced 27 amendments.

It is slow, public, and legitimate. When an amendment passes, nobody can argue about whether it is really the law. It also cannot be quietly undone — reversing one takes another amendment, which has happened exactly once.

Channel two: judicial interpretation

Courts decide what the words mean, and meaning is where most of the action is.

Consider one clause. The Fourteenth Amendment says no state shall deny “the equal protection of the laws.” Those eight words were read in 1896 to permit racially segregated schools. They were read in 1954 to forbid them. Same text, different country.

This channel is fast and low-visibility. A single decision can change the governing rule for the entire country in a morning, and nobody voted on it. It is also reversible — a later Court can undo what an earlier one did, which is exactly what happened between those two decisions, in both directions.

Channel three: practice

Some of the largest changes never went through either of the first two.

The president's power to send troops without a declaration of war is not in the text. Congress has not declared war since 1941, and every major military action since has rested on statutes, resolutions, or claimed presidential authority. That is a constitutional change of enormous size, accomplished by repetition rather than by amendment or ruling.

The same is true of federal agencies. Congress writes a statute saying “clean up the air,” and an agency decides what that means, enforces it, and adjudicates disputes about it. None of that detail is in Article I.

The three, compared

  • Amendment: slowest, hardest, most legitimate, hardest to reverse.
  • Interpretation: faster, contested legitimacy, reversible by a later court or by amendment.
  • Practice: fastest, least visible, easiest to reverse in theory and hardest to reverse in fact.

The necessary and proper clause is the hinge that makes the third channel possible. It lets Congress do what is needed to carry out its powers, which means the reach of federal law expands as the country's needs change.

Why the split matters

It matters because the three channels have different amounts of democratic input.

Amendment requires overwhelming agreement. Interpretation requires five votes. Practice requires only that nobody stop you. A country that assumed change would run through the first channel has increasingly run it through the other two.

That is not automatically bad. Courts sometimes protect people the political system will not. Practice sometimes solves problems faster than legislation could. But it does mean that a great deal of constitutional law was never voted on by anyone, and the country has never fully settled how it feels about that.

Why It Matters

You cannot understand the Constitution by reading only the Constitution. The text is the starting position, not the current one.

This is why the same fourteen words can produce opposite results a lifetime apart, and why two people can both be sincere constitutionalists and disagree completely. They are not usually disagreeing about what the document says. They are disagreeing about which channel should have decided the question, and how much weight a past decision deserves.

The most useful question about any constitutional claim is therefore not “is that in the text?” It is “which channel put it there, and was that the right channel?”
