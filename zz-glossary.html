Glossary

85 entries Linked from the text

Plain definitions for the legal vocabulary this guide uses. Most of these words have a technical meaning that differs from everyday use, which is why they are worth defining once and linking to.

How the document works

Amendment
A formal change to the Constitution, made through the process in Article V. There are 27.
Apportionment
Dividing seats or taxes among the states by population, usually after a census.
Article
One of the seven main divisions of the Constitution, each covering a branch of government or a structural rule.
Checks and balances
The overlapping powers that let each branch block the others: vetoes, overrides, impeachment, judicial review.
Direct tax
A tax on persons or property rather than on transactions. Article I required these to be apportioned by population.
Enumerated powers
The specific powers listed in the Constitution, mostly in Article I, Section 8.
Federalism
The division of power between the national government and the states.
Incorporation
The process by which the Supreme Court applied the Bill of Rights to state governments, one provision at a time, through the Fourteenth Amendment.
Judicial review
The power of courts to strike down laws that conflict with the Constitution. The text does not mention it; the Court claimed it in 1803.
Necessary and proper clause
Article I, Section 8, clause 18. Lets Congress pass laws needed to carry out its listed powers — the legal basis for most federal action.
Political question
An issue courts refuse to decide, leaving it to the elected branches. The Guarantee Clause is the classic example.
Preamble
The opening sentence that states the Constitution's purposes. It grants no powers.
Preemption
When a valid federal law displaces state law on the same subject.
Ratification
Formal approval. The Constitution took effect once nine states ratified it.
Ratification convention
An assembly elected by voters for the specific purpose of voting yes or no on a constitution or amendment.
Selective incorporation
Applying Bill of Rights provisions to the states case by case, only where the right is judged fundamental.
Separation of powers
Splitting lawmaking, enforcement, and judging into three branches so that no single one holds all the power.
Supremacy clause
Article VI. Federal law made under the Constitution overrides conflicting state law.
Total incorporation
The view that the Fourteenth Amendment applies the entire Bill of Rights to the states at once.
Unenumerated rights
Rights that exist but are not listed in the Constitution. The Ninth Amendment says the list is not exhaustive.
“We the People”
The first three words, and the claim that government's authority comes from ordinary citizens rather than from states or a monarch.

Powers and limits

Bill of attainder
A law declaring a specific person guilty and punishing them without a trial. Forbidden.
Commerce clause
Article I, Section 8, clause 3. Lets Congress regulate trade among the states — the source of much modern federal law.
Contracts clause
Article I, Section 10. States may not pass laws impairing the obligation of contracts.
Ex post facto law
A law that punishes conduct that was legal when it happened. Forbidden.
Habeas corpus
The right to have a court check whether your imprisonment is lawful. It can be suspended only in rebellion or invasion.
Impeachment
The process of charging and trying a federal official. The House charges; the Senate tries and may remove.
Pocket veto
When the president does nothing for ten days and Congress adjourns, killing the bill without a written objection.
Quorum
The minimum number of members who must be present for a body to do business. In Congress, a majority.
Recess appointment
The president's power to fill a vacancy while the Senate is out of session. Narrowed sharply by modern courts.
Sovereign immunity
The rule that a state generally cannot be sued without its consent. Created by the Eleventh Amendment.
Taxing and spending power
Congress can raise money and spend it for the general welfare, with conditions attached.
Veto override
Two-thirds of each chamber voting to pass a bill over the president's objection.
“Expressly”
The word in the Articles of Confederation that limited Congress to powers named outright. The Tenth Amendment drops it.
“General Welfare”
A phrase in both the Preamble and Article I, Section 8. Broad enough that its limits have been argued for two centuries.
“Herein granted”
Article I, Section 1. Congress has the powers listed in this document — not whatever it decides it needs.
“In Pursuance thereof”
Article VI. Federal law is supreme only when Congress had the power to pass it in the first place.
“Republican Form of Government”
The Guarantee Clause promise, never defined by the text or clearly enforced by courts.

Rights and courts

Appellate jurisdiction
A court's power to review what a lower court decided. Most of what the Supreme Court hears is appellate.
Birthright citizenship
Citizenship by being born in the country. Established by the Fourteenth Amendment and still contested at its edges.
Cruel and unusual punishment
The Eighth Amendment limit on punishment, read through “evolving standards of decency.”
Diversity jurisdiction
Federal court authority over a dispute between citizens of different states, on the theory that a local court might favor its own.
Double jeopardy
You cannot be tried twice for the same offense by the same government. Separate sovereigns may each prosecute.
Due process of law
Fair legal procedure before the government takes life, liberty, or property. Also read to protect some rights outright.
Equal protection
The Fourteenth Amendment rule that states cannot apply laws unequally to different groups without a good reason.
Establishment clause
The First Amendment rule that government cannot set up or favor an official religion.
Evolving standards of decency
The idea that what counts as cruel and unusual changes as society changes, so the clause's meaning moves without the words changing.
Exclusionary rule
Evidence from an illegal search is usually kept out of trial. This is how the Fourth Amendment gets enforced.
Free exercise clause
The First Amendment rule that government cannot stop you from practicing your religion.
Fundamental right
A right important enough that courts scrutinize laws affecting it very closely.
Grand jury
A panel of citizens that decides whether there is enough evidence to charge someone. It hears only the prosecution.
Lemon test
A three-part test for establishment clause cases used from 1971 to 2022, then set aside in favor of a historical approach.
Original jurisdiction
A court's power to hear a case first rather than on appeal. For the Supreme Court, this covers mainly disputes involving ambassadors or a state.
Peremptory challenge
Striking a juror without giving a reason. Cannot be used to remove jurors by race or sex.
Prior restraint
Government action blocking publication in advance. Courts treat it as almost always unconstitutional.
Privileges or immunities
Two different clauses share this name: Article IV (how states treat outsiders) and the Fourteenth Amendment (narrowed almost to nothing in 1873).
Probable cause
A reasonable basis to believe evidence or contraband will be found — the standard for a warrant.
Reasonable expectation of privacy
The test for whether a search happened at all. If you have no expectation of privacy, there is no search.
Self-incrimination
Being forced to testify against yourself. The Fifth Amendment protects against it.
Substantive due process
The idea that some rights are so fundamental no amount of fair procedure justifies taking them.
Takings clause
The Fifth Amendment rule that government must pay for property it takes for public use.
Unreasonable search
A search or seizure that violates the Fourth Amendment. The text never defines “unreasonable.”
“Subject to the jurisdiction”
The Fourteenth Amendment phrase limiting birthright citizenship. Courts read it to exclude children of foreign diplomats.

Words from the founding

Elector
A person chosen to cast a state's votes for president. Not the same as a voter.
Electoral College
The system of state-appointed electors that formally chooses the president.
Enumerate
To list one by one. Used in the Ninth Amendment and in the census clause.
Extradition
Handing a person accused of a crime over to another state or country.
Lame duck
An official still in office after an election they lost or did not contest.
Militia
A part-time citizen force raised by a state, as distinct from a standing national army.
Posterity
The generations that come after you.
Religious test
A requirement that an officeholder belong to a particular faith. Banned for federal office by Article VI.
“High Crimes and Misdemeanors”
The impeachment standard. Borrowed from English practice; whether it requires an actual crime is disputed.
“Natural born Citizen”
A requirement for the presidency, never defined by the text or squarely resolved by the Supreme Court.
“Well regulated”
In eighteenth-century usage: properly organized, equipped, and drilled. Not “heavily regulated by law.”

History and cases

Anti-Federalists
Opponents of ratification, who argued the document centralized too much power and lacked a bill of rights.
Articles of Confederation
America's first constitution, in force 1781–1789. A league of states with no taxing power, no president, and no national courts.
Barron v. Baltimore
The 1833 decision holding that the Bill of Rights did not apply to the states.
Convict leasing
The practice of leasing imprisoned people to private employers — a system built on the Thirteenth Amendment's criminal-punishment exception.
District of Columbia v. Heller
The 2008 decision recognizing an individual right to keep a firearm, unconnected to militia service.
Dred Scott v. Sandford
The 1857 decision denying citizenship to Black Americans. Overturned by the Fourteenth Amendment.
Federalist Papers
85 essays by Hamilton, Madison, and Jay arguing for ratification, published 1787–88.
Gideon v. Wainwright
The 1963 decision requiring states to provide a lawyer to poor defendants in felony cases.
Great Compromise
The 1787 deal creating a House apportioned by population and a Senate with two seats per state.
Marbury v. Madison
The 1803 decision establishing judicial review.
Miranda v. Arizona
The 1966 decision requiring police to warn suspects of their rights before questioning.
Shays' Rebellion
A 1786 farmers' uprising in Massachusetts that exposed how powerless the national government was.
Slaughter-House Cases
The 1873 decision that read the Fourteenth Amendment's privileges or immunities clause almost out of existence.
Three-Fifths Compromise
The 1787 bargain counting enslaved people as three-fifths of a person for representation. Replaced by the Fourteenth Amendment.
