Part Five · Putting It Together

Separation of Powers, Traced

The idea everything else assumes 6 min read

In the Text

Plain English

Three sentences, opening three articles. Read them together and you can see the whole design in about eighty words.

Notice what is different about the first one. Article I says herein granted — Congress gets the powers listed, and no more. Articles II and III have no such qualifier. That asymmetry is the seed of every argument about executive and judicial overreach.

Why separate the powers at all

Not for efficiency. Dividing authority among three bodies that must cooperate is a slow, frustrating way to run anything.

The framers separated power because they did not trust concentration. James Madison put the argument in Federalist No. 51: the way to keep the branches in their lanes is to give each one the means to resist the others. Ambition is made to counteract ambition. The system assumes everyone will push for more power, and it turns that into a safeguard.

The checks, traced through the text

Congress over the president. It can override a veto with two-thirds of each chamber (Art. I, § 7). It confirms or rejects appointments and treaties (Art. II, § 2). It can impeach and remove (Art. I, § 2 and § 3). It funds — or does not fund — anything the president wants to do (Art. I, § 9).

The president over Congress. A veto returns a bill with objections (Art. I, § 7). A pocket veto kills it silently when Congress adjourns. The president also sets the agenda through the State of the Union and controls how aggressively laws are enforced.

The courts over both. Courts can declare a law or an executive action unconstitutional. That power is not written down; it was claimed in 1803 and accepted ever since.

Congress over the courts. It creates the lower courts, sets the number of justices, and can strip cases out of the Supreme Court's appellate docket (Art. III, § 2). It can impeach judges. And it can amend the Constitution to overturn a ruling.

The president over the courts. The president nominates every federal judge, and can pardon people convicted under federal law.

Where the design strains

Three things the framers did not plan for have pulled the system out of shape.

Political parties. The Constitution does not mention them. The framers assumed branches would compete. In practice, a president and a congressional majority from the same party do not check each other — they cooperate. When the same party holds both, the separation of powers mostly stops functioning.

Delegation. Congress increasingly writes broad statutes and lets federal agencies fill in the rules. Those agencies make rules, enforce them, and decide disputes about them — all three functions in one body. Whether that is consistent with the structure is a live question at the Supreme Court.

Executive growth. War, foreign policy, and the sheer size of the federal government have moved real power toward the presidency. Congress still passes the laws. It has increasingly stopped deciding what they mean.

Why It Matters

Separation of powers is the load-bearing idea in the whole document. Rights are listed in the Bill of Rights, but they are enforced by a structure. If one branch could make, execute, and judge the law, the list of rights would be a list of suggestions.

It is also the part of the Constitution most often taken for granted. Nobody argues that the three branches should be merged. But the checks only work when the branches are actually independent — and they are independent only as long as the people staffing them are willing to act that way.

That is the fragile part. The text creates the offices and the powers. It cannot create the willingness to use them against your own party. Every generation has to supply that itself.
