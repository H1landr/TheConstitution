Part Two · The Seven Articles

Article VII — Ratification

One sentence Nine states, not thirteen 5 min read

In the Text

Plain English

Twenty-five words, and every one of them was a political decision.

Nine, not thirteen

The Articles of Confederation could only be changed with every state's agreement. That rule had just spent a decade paralyzing the country.

So the framers wrote a new rule. Nine states would be enough. The Constitution would take effect among those nine, whether or not the other four ever agreed.

That was legally shaky. They had been sent to Philadelphia to amend the Articles, not to replace them, and the Articles themselves said unanimity was required. A strict reading makes the whole convention an unauthorized act.

The framers knew it. They gambled that a working government, once running, would pull the holdouts in. The gamble worked. North Carolina ratified in November 1789 and Rhode Island in May 1790, both after the new government had already started operating without them.

Conventions, not legislatures

This is the detail that gets skipped, and it is the most important one. The Constitution was not submitted to state legislatures. It was submitted to specially elected state conventions.

There was a practical reason. State legislators would have been asked to vote away their own authority. Many would have refused on instinct, and several states made it clear they would.

But there was also a theoretical reason, and it is bigger. A convention speaks for the people of the state directly. A legislature speaks for the state government. Routing ratification through conventions let the framers argue that the Constitution came from the people, not from the states. That is the same claim the Preamble makes with its first three words.

That move has never fully satisfied everyone. The Constitution was ratified state by state, not by a single national vote, which means it is a compact among peoples rather than an act of one people. Both readings have been argued for two centuries.

How the fight went

Delaware was first, on December 7, 1787, five days after the convention adjourned. Pennsylvania followed within a week, after a fight loud enough that delegates were physically dragged to the chamber to make a quorum.

Massachusetts came in February 1788, narrowly, and only after the Federalists agreed to recommend amendments. That concession set the pattern: ratification came with an implied promise of a bill of rights.

New Hampshire became the ninth state on June 21, 1788, which triggered the Constitution's establishment. Virginia ratified four days later and New York a month after that, both with long lists of proposed amendments attached.

Virginia's debate was the heavyweight match. Patrick Henry argued against ratification for days. James Madison answered him. Henry lost by 89 votes to 79.

The one that came last

Rhode Island never sent delegates to Philadelphia and did not ratify until May 1790. By then it was the only state outside the union, and Congress was preparing to treat its goods as foreign imports. It approved the Constitution by 34 votes to 32, with a bill of rights demand attached.

So the document that begins “We the People of the United States” was ratified by eleven states before the other two joined. One of them needed economic pressure to do it.

When it started

The Constitution took effect on March 4, 1789, when the first Congress convened. George Washington was inaugurated on April 30, 1789. The Confederation Congress dissolved quietly in between.

Why It Matters

Article VII is the article about legitimacy. Every other article tells you what the government may do. This one tells you where the government's authority came from in the first place.

Its answer is: from elected conventions of the people, in nine states, with the rest following. That is a procedural answer to a philosophical question, and it is the answer the country has lived with ever since.

It also established a habit that runs through the whole document. When the rules got in the way of a decision the framers thought was necessary, they changed the rules rather than abandon the decision. The unanimity requirement was inconvenient, so they wrote a new threshold. Whether that counts as wisdom or as a coup is a question Americans have never quite stopped asking.
