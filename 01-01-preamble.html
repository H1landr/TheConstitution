Part One · The Preamble

“We the People”

One sentence 52 words 3 min read

In the Text

Plain English

That is the whole preamble. One sentence, six stated purposes, and a claim about where the government's authority comes from.

The claim comes first, and it is the reason the sentence is famous. In 1787, nearly every government on earth took its power from a king, an emperor, or a church. This one starts from the opposite direction: We the People. The authority to build a government sits with ordinary people, and the government holds that authority on loan.

Ordain just means to establish formally. So the sentence reads: we, the people, are officially setting up this Constitution. The people are doing the ordaining. Not the states, not the delegates in Philadelphia — the people.

Everything after the comma is a list of reasons. Six of them, each one a promise about what the new government is for.

A more perfect Union. The states were already joined together, badly, under the Articles of Confederation. That arrangement was so weak the country could barely collect taxes or settle disputes between states. “More perfect” means better than what they had — not flawless, just improved. It is an odd word to use about a country, and it was chosen carefully. The Union was a work in progress.

Justice. Courts, laws, and rules that apply the same way every time, so that a dispute gets settled by a judge instead of a fight.

Domestic tranquility. Peace at home. This was not abstract. Months earlier, farmers in western Massachusetts had risen up in what became known as Shays' Rebellion. The national government had no power to raise troops or money to stop them. “Tranquility” was on everybody's mind that summer.

The common defence. One shared military, instead of thirteen separate ones with thirteen separate budgets.

The general welfare. Conditions in which people can do well. This phrase is broad enough that Americans have argued about its limits for over two hundred years, and they are still arguing.

The blessings of liberty, to ourselves and our posterity. Freedom that lasts longer than the people who wrote it down. Posterity means the generations that come after you.

Who counted as “We”

The preamble does not say “all people,” and in 1787 the gap between the promise and the reality was enormous. Women could not vote anywhere. Enslaved people were counted as three-fifths of a person for the purpose of representation, and had no rights the law would protect. Most states limited voting to white men who owned property. Native nations were treated as foreign countries, not as part of the “We” at all.

So the preamble was not a description of America in 1787. It was a standard the country would spend the next two centuries being held to — usually by the very people the document had left out.

Why It Matters

The preamble gives the government no powers. It grants nothing and prohibits nothing. Read literally, it is a statement of purpose attached to the front of a rulebook, like the mission line at the top of a contract.

That does not make it decoration. When a clause later in the document is ambiguous, courts and legislators go back to the preamble to ask what the whole thing was for. It is the document's answer to the question “why are we doing any of this?”

There is also a quieter point buried in the word “more.” The men who wrote this sentence did not think they were handing down a finished thing. They wrote “more perfect,” not “perfect.” Then they built Article V — the article that explains how to amend the whole document — because they expected it to need changing. The preamble's most honest word is the smallest one.
