Part Two · The Seven Articles

Article II — The President

Four sections The shortest of the three branches 9 min read

In the Text

Plain English

Short on purpose

Article I takes about 2,300 words to describe Congress. Article II takes roughly 1,000 to describe the president. The framers were not careless. They were nervous.

They had just fought a war against a king. Many delegates at the convention did not want a single executive at all, and some wanted a committee instead. The compromise was one president, but a short leash.

That brevity turned out to be the most consequential thing about Article II. It left a lot undefined, and undefined power tends to grow.

Section 1 — The Electoral College

The president is not chosen by a national popular vote. Each state gets a number of electors equal to its total seats in Congress — its House seats plus exactly two, because every state gets two senators.

That formula builds in a small-state advantage. California has 54 electors for roughly 39 million people. Wyoming has 3 for roughly 580,000. Wyoming voters therefore carry far more electoral weight per person.

Two things about the system surprise people. First, winner-take-all is not in the Constitution. It is a rule states invented. Maine and Nebraska currently split their electors by congressional district instead. Second, electors were supposed to be independent judges of character. In practice they are chosen by parties and vote as pledged.

The original design also made the runner-up the vice president. That worked once. Then the election of 1800 produced a tie between Thomas Jefferson and his own running mate, and the system nearly broke. The Twelfth Amendment fixed it in 1804 by putting president and vice president on separate ballots.

One more inheritance worth naming. Because enslaved people counted as three-fifths of a person for apportionment, slaveholding states received extra House seats and therefore extra electors. Those seats belonged to people who could not vote at all.

Section 1 — Who can be president

You must be at least 35 years old, a resident of the United States for 14 years, and a natural born Citizen. That last phrase has never been tested at the Supreme Court. It is generally read to mean “a citizen from birth,” which is why candidates born abroad to American parents have been treated as eligible.

The Constitution sets no limit on how many terms a president may serve. The two-term limit came much later, with the Twenty-Second Amendment in 1951, after Franklin Roosevelt won four elections.

Section 1 — The oath

This is the only oath the Constitution spells out word for word. Members of Congress and federal judges take oaths too, but their wording is left to law.

Notice what is being promised. Not to defend the country, and not to serve the people. To preserve, protect, and defend the Constitution. The document makes itself the object of the president's loyalty.

Section 2 — Commander in chief

The president commands the Army and Navy, and the state militias when they are called into federal service. That sounds sweeping. Read alongside Article I, it is narrower than it sounds, because Congress declares war and Congress pays for the military.

The design was a split: Congress decides whether there is a war, and the president runs it. The last time Congress formally declared war was in 1941. Every major military action since — Korea, Vietnam, the Gulf War, Afghanistan, Iraq — has run on statutes, resolutions, or claimed presidential authority instead. Where the line sits is genuinely contested.

Section 2 — Pardons

The president can grant reprieves and pardons for federal crimes, with one exception: cases of impeachment. A pardon cannot rescue someone from being removed from office.

It is a broad power. It reaches federal convictions, not state ones, and it cannot be overridden by Congress. Whether a president may pardon himself is an open question. No court has ever ruled on it.

Section 2 — Treaties and appointments

Two shared powers, and the details matter.

Treaties need the consent of two-thirds of the senators present — not two-thirds of the whole Senate. That is a high bar, and it is why the United States often governs its foreign relationships through executive agreements instead.

Appointments — ambassadors, judges, and senior officials — need only a simple majority. Congress may hand the appointment of lower-level officers to the president alone, to the courts, or to department heads.

The president may also fill vacancies while the Senate is in recess, with commissions that expire when the next session ends. This recess appointment power was once routine and has been narrowed sharply by the courts in recent decades.

One thing is missing entirely: the word cabinet never appears. Section 2 lets the president demand written opinions from the heads of departments, and that is all. The cabinet is a custom, not a constitutional body.

Section 3 — The duty to execute the laws

Ten words, and the most important ones in the article. This clause is the legal basis for the entire executive branch as we know it — federal agencies, prosecutors, regulations, executive orders.

Section 3 also requires the president to report to Congress on the state of the union. It gives the power to convene or adjourn Congress in narrow circumstances. And it makes the president the official who receives foreign ambassadors. That last duty is the source of the power to recognize foreign governments.

The open question hidden in “faithfully executed” is whether a president may decline to enforce a law he believes is unconstitutional. Every administration has claimed some version of that power. Its limits have never been settled.

Section 4 — Impeachment

The president, vice president, and all civil officers may be removed for treason, bribery, or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors.

That last phrase is the whole argument. It is a term borrowed from English impeachment practice, where it meant serious abuse of public office — not necessarily a crime in the criminal code. Some scholars read it broadly as covering any grave misconduct. Others insist it means indictable offenses. The House has impeached and the Senate has tried officials under both readings.

Why It Matters

Article II is why the American presidency is the most powerful elected office in the world and also the hardest to describe precisely. The text gives a title, a few named powers, and a duty to execute the laws. It does not define the limits.

Two sentences get argued about constantly. “The executive Power shall be vested in a President” can be read as a general grant of authority over anything executive in nature. Or it can be read as a label for the powers listed afterward. The first reading supports a strong presidency. The second supports a president confined to what the article enumerates.

Almost every modern fight about presidential power — over war, over spending, over investigations, over emergency declarations — is a fight between those two readings. The Constitution does not resolve it. It provides the language and leaves the argument to the branches.
