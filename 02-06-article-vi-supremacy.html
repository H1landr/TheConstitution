Part Two · The Seven Articles

Article VI — Supremacy

Three clauses The most quoted sentence in the document 5 min read

In the Text

Plain English

What the supremacy clause actually says

Three things are supreme: the Constitution, federal laws made under it, and treaties. Where they conflict with state law, they win.

That is the clause that turns fifty legal systems into one country. Without it, a state could pass a law nullifying a federal one and there would be no clear answer about which applied.

The clause is addressed to judges specifically. That is not incidental. State judges hear the overwhelming majority of cases in America, and the framers needed those judges to apply federal law even when their own state's law said otherwise. The clause tells them which document controls.

The four words everyone skips

In Pursuance thereof. Federal law is supreme only when it is made in pursuance of the Constitution — in other words, only when Congress had the authority to pass it in the first place.

This is the limit that gets forgotten in most arguments. A federal statute that exceeds Congress's enumerated powers is not supreme law. It is not law at all. The supremacy clause settles which law wins when both are valid. It does not make Congress unlimited.

That is why the fight over federal power almost always starts with Article I, Section 8, and not with Article VI. The question is never really “does federal law beat state law?” The question is “did Congress have the power to pass this?”

How it works in practice

When federal and state law collide, courts say the federal law preempts the state one. There are three flavors.

Express preemption is when a statute says so directly: this law replaces state law on this subject.

Field preemption is when Congress regulates an area so thoroughly that no room is left for states to act.

Conflict preemption is when complying with both laws is impossible, or when state law obstructs the federal purpose.

Outside those situations, states are free to legislate — often more strictly than the federal government does. States can set a higher minimum wage, tougher environmental rules, or stricter gun laws, as long as they are not in direct conflict with federal law.

Treaties

Treaties are listed as supreme law alongside statutes, which is unusual internationally. A ratified treaty binds the states directly.

In practice, courts distinguish between treaties that apply automatically and those that require Congress to pass implementing legislation first. Most trade and human rights treaties fall in the second category, which softens the clause considerably.

The other two clauses

The debts clause says obligations incurred under the Articles of Confederation remain valid under the new Constitution. It reads like housekeeping and is actually a promise to creditors. The new government was telling bondholders it would not repudiate the old government's debts, which made it possible to borrow.

The religious test clause is the one worth pausing on. No religious Test shall ever be required as a Qualification to any Office or public Trust under the United States. That is the only reference to religion in the original Constitution, and it is a prohibition.

It was a radical thing to write in 1787. Most states still had religious tests of their own — bars on Catholics, Jews, and non-Christians holding office. Several kept them for decades afterward. The federal clause applied only to federal offices, but it announced a principle: belief is not a qualification for public service.

Why It Matters

Article VI is short and does an enormous amount of work. It establishes that the United States is one country with one supreme legal order, not a treaty organization of fifty sovereigns. Almost every federalism case in American law is a fight about how far that order reaches.

It is also the article that makes the rest of the document enforceable. A Constitution that state judges could ignore would be advice. Article VI is what makes it law.

And the religious test clause is a reminder that not everything important in the Constitution is long. Nine words, tucked into a paragraph about oaths, settled that the United States would not require a particular faith from the people who run it.
