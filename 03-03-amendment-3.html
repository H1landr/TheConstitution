Part Three · The Bill of Rights

Amendment 3 — Quartering

Ratified 1791 The shortest amendment 3 min read

In the Text

Plain English

Soldiers cannot be housed in your home without your permission in peacetime. In wartime, Congress has to pass a law saying how it works.

This is the least-used amendment in the Constitution. It has never been the basis for a Supreme Court decision striking down a law. It has been cited in a handful of lower court cases, most notably a 1982 appeals ruling about National Guard members housed in a correctional officers' dormitory during a strike.

That is roughly the whole judicial record.

Why it exists

Because it had already happened. The Quartering Act of 1765 required colonial legislatures to supply barracks and provisions for British troops. A second version in 1774 went further and allowed troops to be housed in occupied buildings, including private ones.

The Declaration of Independence lists it among the grievances: “Quartering large bodies of armed troops among us.”

So this amendment is a direct response to a specific injury the colonists had lived through. It went into the Bill of Rights along with the others, and then the circumstances it addressed stopped occurring.

Why it never mattered much

The United States has never fought a major war on its own soil while maintaining a large standing army that needed civilian housing. There were no barracks crises after 1791. The problem the amendment solved simply did not come back.

Every other amendment in the Bill of Rights got tested constantly, because the situations they describe recur — speech, searches, trials, punishment. Nobody has proposed quartering troops in a private home in two hundred years.

Why It Matters

A dead amendment still says something. The Third Amendment establishes that the home is a distinct kind of space, one the government cannot simply occupy.

That principle did not stay here. It is the same idea behind the Fourth Amendment's protection of “houses” against unreasonable searches, and behind the privacy reasoning courts have built on top of it. The Third Amendment is where the thought first appears in the document, in its most physical form: the government cannot put its people in your rooms.

It is also a useful reminder about how constitutions age. The framers wrote against the specific abuses they remembered. Most of those abuses never returned. A few of them returned in forms nobody in 1791 could have predicted, and the language had to be stretched to cover them. The Third Amendment is the honest case where the problem really did go away.
