Part Four · The Amendments Since 1791

Amendment 11 — Suing a State

Ratified 1795 The fastest amendment 4 min read

In the Text

Plain English

This is the only amendment that takes power away from someone. It says you generally cannot sue a state in federal court.

What happened in 1793

A South Carolina merchant named Alexander Chisholm was the executor of a Georgia estate. He sued Georgia in the Supreme Court, seeking payment for goods supplied during the Revolution. Georgia refused to show up.

The Court ruled 4–1 that Article III gave it jurisdiction over a suit against a state by a citizen of another state. Georgia had to answer.

Georgia's reaction was extreme. Its legislature passed a bill stating that anyone who tried to carry out the ruling would be guilty of a felony and suffer death by hanging. There is no evidence most of the state was calmer about it.

Congress proposed this amendment the next year. It was ratified in 1795 — the fastest turnaround of any amendment in American history.

What it actually does

It creates what lawyers call sovereign immunity. A state cannot be dragged into federal court by a private party without its consent.

The text says “Citizens of another State.” In 1890, the Supreme Court read it much more broadly, holding that a state also cannot be sued by its own citizens. That is not what the sentence says, and it remains a source of argument.

The exceptions

There are several, and they matter more in practice than the rule.

A state can consent to being sued. Many do, for routine contract disputes.

You can sue a state official rather than the state itself, asking a court to order them to stop doing something unlawful. This 1908 workaround is the single most important exception, and a large share of cases against state agencies run through it.

Congress can also override state immunity when it acts under certain amendments — notably the enforcement sections of the Fourteenth Amendment.

Why It Matters

The Eleventh Amendment is the first proof that the amendment process can work quickly. When the states were angry enough about something, they fixed it in a year.

It also establishes an idea that runs through American law: governments are not ordinary defendants. A state can break a contract or injure someone and be immune from suit, not because it did nothing wrong, but because of its status.

That is a genuinely uncomfortable principle in a system that claims to guarantee equal treatment under law. The counterargument is that states are not private companies. They are the entities that create the courts. Subjecting them to suit, on this view, would drain public funds through litigation rather than through politics. Both views have serious defenders.
