In the Text

Plain English

That is the fine print at the bottom of the last page. It contains the date, and it contains a small piece of spin.

Unanimous Consent of the States present. Read those last two words carefully. It does not say everyone agreed. It says every state that was in the room agreed.

Rhode Island never sent anyone. Three delegates who were there — Elbridge Gerry of Massachusetts, George Mason of Virginia, and Edmund Randolph of Virginia — refused to sign. So the document was not unanimous in any ordinary sense, and the men who wrote that phrase knew it.

The room

Fifty-five delegates attended at various points over four months. Thirty-nine signed. They met in the same building where the Declaration of Independence had been adopted eleven years earlier, now called Independence Hall.

They met in secret. Windows were shut in a Philadelphia summer. Delegates agreed that nothing said in the room would be repeated outside it. The reason was practical: if every floated idea became public, no one could change their mind without humiliation. Secrecy let them argue honestly and then compromise.

Almost everything we know about what happened inside comes from James Madison's notes, published after his death. He sat near the front and wrote continuously. Without him, the convention would be close to a blank.

They were only supposed to revise

Congress had called the convention to fix the Articles of Confederation. The instructions were to propose amendments. Within about two weeks, the delegates had decided to throw the Articles out and write a new government from scratch.

That was well beyond what they had been authorized to do. It is the single most important fact about the summer. It also explains why the finished document ends with Article VII — a ratification process that skips the state legislatures entirely.

The four compromises

Large states versus small states. Virginia wanted representation by population. New Jersey wanted one vote per state. The deadlock broke in July with the Great Compromise, sometimes called the Connecticut Compromise. The House is apportioned by population. The Senate gives every state two seats regardless of size.

How to count enslaved people. Southern states wanted enslaved people counted for representation, since that would give them more seats — while still treating those same people as property. Northern delegates objected. The result was the Three-Fifths Compromise: enslaved people counted as three-fifths of a person for apportionment. Nobody pretended this was just. It was a deal to keep the negotiations alive.

The slave trade. Several states wanted Congress to be able to ban the importation of enslaved people immediately. Georgia and South Carolina refused to sign anything that allowed it. The bargain: Congress could not prohibit the trade until 1808. It banned it in 1808, the first year it legally could.

How to choose a president. Some delegates wanted Congress to pick the president. Others wanted a direct popular vote. The Electoral College was the compromise nobody loved: a temporary group of electors, chosen by each state as it sees fit, who cast the actual votes.

The fight after the summer

Signing was the easy part. Nine states had to ratify, and the country split into two camps.

The Federalists supported the Constitution. Alexander Hamilton, James Madison, and John Jay wrote eighty-five essays under the pen name “Publius,” now called the Federalist Papers, arguing for it.

The Anti-Federalists opposed it. Patrick Henry, George Mason, and others argued that the document moved too much power to a distant capital. They said a president looked too much like a king. They objected that the document contained no bill of rights.

The Anti-Federalists lost the vote but won the argument. Ratification came with an understanding that the new government would add protections for individual rights. The first Congress proposed twelve amendments in 1789. Ten were ratified by 1791, and they became the Bill of Rights.

The two that failed did not disappear. One of them, about congressional pay, sat unratified for two centuries and finally became the Twenty-Seventh Amendment in 1992.

Why It Matters

The Constitution is not a philosophy that fell from the sky. It is a settlement among people who disagreed sharply and needed a deal. Almost every strange feature of the document traces back to a specific argument in a specific week.

That is why the Senate gives Wyoming the same say as California — a small-state demand. It is why the Electoral College exists — a distrust of both Congress and direct democracy. It is why the words “slave” and “slavery” never appear in the original text, only careful circumlocutions — a bargain struck to hold the union together.

Knowing the bargains makes the compromises visible, including the ones that were indefensible. The men in that room knew exactly what they were doing with the Three-Fifths Clause. Several said so out loud. Two generations later, the country had to fight a civil war and pass three amendments to undo that part of the deal.
