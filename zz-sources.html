The text

Every quoted clause in this guide comes from the National Archives, transcribed from the parchment itself. Nothing is quoted from memory.

  • The Constitution. National Archives, The Constitution of the United States: A Transcription. archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript
  • The Bill of Rights. National Archives, The Bill of Rights: A Transcription. archives.gov/founding-docs/bill-of-rights-transcript
  • Amendments 11–27. National Archives, The Constitution: Amendments 11-27. archives.gov/founding-docs/amendments-11-27
  • The Articles of Confederation. National Archives, Articles of Confederation (1777). archives.gov/milestone-documents/articles-of-confederation

The Archives transcriptions preserve the original spelling, capitalization, and punctuation — “defence,” “chuse,” “Posterity.” This guide keeps them exactly. Where a modern writer would quietly correct something, this one does not.

How the quotes were checked

Each quoted passage was extracted from the finished page and compared character by character against the source text, after normalizing whitespace and quotation marks. Truncated quotes — marked with an ellipsis — were checked as fragments of the original rather than in full.

Where a quote was verified and did not match, the page was corrected rather than the quotation adjusted. Several pages went through that.

Pages that describe moving law

Constitutional law is not static, and a few pages describe rules that are actively changing. These are the pages most likely to go stale, and the ones to re-check first:

  • Amendment 1 — the establishment-clause test was replaced in 2022 and the replacement is still being worked out.
  • Amendment 2 — the historical-tradition test from 2022 is producing new decisions continuously.
  • Amendment 8 — the scope of “cruel and unusual” and of excessive fines is still developing.
  • Amendment 14 — birthright citizenship and the disqualification clause have both been litigated recently.
  • Amendment 24 and Amendment 15 — election-law questions around these provisions are active.
  • Part Five — the descriptions of federalism and judicial power are summaries of a moving target.

What this is not

This is a reading guide, not legal advice. It is not a treatise, and it does not try to survey the scholarship. Where a question is genuinely contested, this guide says so rather than resolving it, which means some pages will feel unsatisfying to a reader who wants an answer.

It is written for a ninth-grade reading level. That means some nuance is compressed. If a page here is the only thing you read about a topic, you will have a decent map and an incomplete one.

How this was built

Plain HTML, one stylesheet, one JavaScript file. No build step, no framework, no dependencies, no tracking. It runs by opening a file in a browser and works offline. The table of contents lives in a single array in app.js, so the whole site's structure is one editable list.
