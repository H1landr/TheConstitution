Part Three · The Bill of Rights

How It Reached the States

1833 – 2019 The missing 130 years 6 min read

In the Text

Plain English

The word that matters

Congress shall make no law. Not “government.” Not “no state.” Congress.

Every right on the previous ten pages was written to limit the federal government. None of them said anything about your state, your county, your town, or the officer who pulls you over.

That is not a loophole someone invented later. It is the plain text, and the Supreme Court confirmed it early.

Barron v. Baltimore, 1833

John Barron owned a wharf in Baltimore harbor. The city dumped street construction debris into the water, and the resulting silt made his wharf unusable. Barron sued, arguing that the city had taken his property without just compensation — a Fifth Amendment violation.

Chief Justice John Marshall, writing for a unanimous Court, said no. The Fifth Amendment's takings clause restricts the federal government. It has nothing to say about a city. Barron lost.

So for the first century and a half of American history, a state could censor its newspapers and search homes without warrants. It could hold trials without lawyers. The Bill of Rights offered no federal remedy for any of it.

The Fourteenth Amendment opens a door

In 1868, after the Civil War, the Fourteenth Amendment changed the grammar of the document. Where the First Amendment said Congress shall make no law, the Fourteenth said No State shall.

That phrase appears three times in one sentence, attached to three protections: privileges or immunities, due process, and equal protection.

One road closed

The most natural route was the first one. States cannot abridge the privileges or immunities of citizens of the United States. If the Bill of Rights defines those privileges, then the whole Bill of Rights applies to the states at once.

In 1873, five years after ratification, the Supreme Court shut that door in the Slaughter-House Cases. It read the clause narrowly, limiting it to a few rights of national citizenship such as the right to travel between states. The clause has been nearly useless ever since.

The other road: due process, one right at a time

So the work got done through the due process clause instead, and it was done slowly. A right was applied to the states only after a case arose in which a state had violated it. It also required the Court to conclude the right was fundamental to ordered liberty.

This process is called incorporation. It took roughly seventy years, and it went like this:

  • 1897 — the takings clause, in a case about a railroad.
  • 1925 — free speech, assumed for the first time without deciding the question.
  • 1931 — freedom of the press.
  • 1932 — the right to a lawyer, in a capital case.
  • 1937 — assembly.
  • 1940 — free exercise of religion.
  • 1947 — the establishment clause.
  • 1961 — the exclusionary rule for illegal searches.
  • 1963 — the right to appointed counsel in felony cases.
  • 1964 — the privilege against self-incrimination.
  • 1965 — confronting witnesses.
  • 1968 — the right to a jury trial.
  • 2010 — the right to keep and bear arms.
  • 2019 — the ban on excessive fines.

What was never incorporated

Four provisions remain outside. The Third Amendment's ban on quartering soldiers has never been applied to the states. Neither has the Fifth Amendment's grand jury requirement. Nor the Seventh Amendment's right to a civil jury. And the Eighth Amendment's excessive bail clause has not been squarely decided.

Note that the 2010 and 2019 entries are recent. This process is not finished, and it is not purely historical.

The argument about method

Two justices disagreed about how to do this, and the disagreement never went away.

Justice Hugo Black argued for total incorporation: the Fourteenth Amendment applies the entire Bill of Rights to the states, all at once, with no picking and choosing.

Justice Felix Frankfurter argued for selective incorporation: courts should apply a provision only if it is fundamental to a fair system, judged case by case.

Frankfurter's approach won. In practice the outcome has converged on Black's, since nearly everything has now been incorporated. But the method still matters, because it leaves judges deciding what counts as fundamental.

Why It Matters

This is the most important thing on any of these eleven pages, and it is almost always left out of civics material. When you learn the Bill of Rights in school, you are usually taught that it protects you from the government. For the first 130 years of the country, it protected you from one government and not the one you were most likely to meet.

Every case in this project that involves a local police officer, a public school, or a state law depends on the Fourteenth Amendment, not on the amendment being quoted. Free speech at a state university is protected by incorporation. So is the warning on your Miranda rights, and the lawyer appointed for a felony defendant in a state court.

None of that was true in 1900. All of it is true now, and none of it came from an amendment that anyone voted on recently. It came from a series of court decisions reading one clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

Which brings the argument full circle. The Bill of Rights is often described as the country's protection against government overreach. The more accurate statement is that a set of limits on Congress became a set of limits on every government. That took seventy years, and it happened by judicial decision rather than by amendment.
