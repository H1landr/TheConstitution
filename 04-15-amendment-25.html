Part Four · The Amendments Since 1791

Amendment 25 — Succession

Ratified 1967 Four sections, one untested 5 min read

In the Text

Plain English

Four sections answering a question the Constitution had handled badly for 178 years: what happens when a president cannot serve?

Section 1: the vice president becomes president

This sounds obvious and was genuinely ambiguous. Article II said the powers and duties of the office “shall devolve on the Vice President.” It did not say the vice president became president.

When William Henry Harrison died in 1841, Vice President John Tyler simply took the oath and moved into the office. Everyone objected. Some called him “His Accidency.” But he did it, and the precedent stuck.

Section 1 turned that precedent into text. A vice president who takes over becomes president outright, not an acting president.

Section 2: filling a vice presidential vacancy

Before this, there was no mechanism. If the vice president died or resigned, the office stayed empty until the next election.

Now the president nominates a replacement, and both houses of Congress must confirm by majority vote. It has been used twice, both times in the 1970s. Spiro Agnew resigned in 1973 and Gerald Ford was confirmed. When Nixon resigned in 1974, Ford became president and nominated Nelson Rockefeller.

For a stretch during that period, neither the president nor the vice president had been elected to their offices.

Section 3: the president declares inability

The president can voluntarily transfer power by written declaration to the leaders of Congress. The vice president acts as president until the president sends a written declaration that the inability has ended.

This has been used briefly several times, usually around surgery or a medical procedure — most notably in 1985 and in 2002 and 2007.

Section 4: the untested one

This section handles the case where the president cannot or will not declare their own inability.

The vice president and a majority of the cabinet can send a written declaration that the president is unable to discharge the duties of the office. The vice president then acts as president.

If the president disputes it, Congress decides. A two-thirds vote of both houses is required to keep the vice president in charge. Otherwise the president resumes.

Section 4 has never been used. It is the only major provision in the Constitution that is entirely untested in practice.

Why It Matters

For most of American history, presidential disability was governed by luck. Woodrow Wilson was incapacitated by a stroke in 1919 and his wife effectively managed the office for the remainder of his term. No one told the public how bad it was.

The Twenty-Fifth Amendment closed that gap, mostly. It gave the country a procedure for a president who is unconscious, undergoing surgery, or otherwise unable to function.

What it did not solve is the political case. Section 4 was written imagining a president who is medically unable. It does not clearly address a president who is capable of making decisions but is making bad ones. That is a political problem, and the amendment deliberately does not treat it as a medical one.
