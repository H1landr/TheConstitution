Part Four · The Amendments Since 1791

Amendment 17 — Electing Senators

Ratified 1913 Changed the structure, not the rights 5 min read

In the Text

Plain English

Four words carry the change: elected by the people. Before 1913, the people did not elect senators. State legislatures did.

The original design

Article I, Section 3 said senators were “chosen by the Legislature” of each state. That was deliberate. The House represented people; the Senate represented state governments. Giving state legislatures a direct voice in Congress was part of how the framers balanced federal and state power.

It worked as designed for about a century, and then it started to fail in visible ways.

What went wrong

Deadlocks. If a legislature could not agree, the seat stayed empty. Delaware left a Senate seat vacant for four years around 1900. Several other states had long vacancies.

Corruption. Legislative caucuses sometimes sold the seat. Several high-profile bribery scandals in the late nineteenth century made national news, and the phrase “millionaire's club” attached itself to the Senate.

Gridlock that blocked everything else. When a legislature spent months fighting over a Senate seat, it did not do its other work.

The workaround that became the rule

Oregon invented a fix in 1904. The state held a popular vote on who should be senator, and the legislature agreed in advance to appoint whoever won. This was called the Oregon system, and other states copied it.

By 1912, the Senate was being chosen this way in a large share of states, while the Constitution still said legislatures did it. The amendment simply made the practice official.

The mechanics

Section 1 changes the election. Section 2 lets a governor make a temporary appointment when a seat opens mid-term, but only if the state legislature allows it, and the appointee must be replaced by election.

That vacancy clause still matters. It is why Senate vacancies are filled by appointment in most states and by special election in a few.

Why It Matters

This is the only amendment that changed the structure of Congress. Every other amendment added a right or fixed a procedure. This one changed who the Senate answers to.

Before 1913, a senator's constituents were the members of a state legislature. After, they were the voters. That is a completely different job, and senators began behaving differently almost immediately — more public campaigning, more attention to popular opinion, less loyalty to statehouse leadership.

Some critics argue the change weakened federalism. If the Senate no longer represents state governments, they say, the states lost their institutional voice in Washington. Defenders answer that the old system was corrupt and paralyzed, and that a theory of federalism which produced four-year vacancies was not worth preserving.

Note one thing the amendment does not do. It did not change the two-senators-per-state rule. Wyoming still gets the same Senate representation as California. That was deliberately locked in Article V, which says no state can lose its equal Senate vote without consenting.
