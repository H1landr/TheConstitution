Part Four · The Amendments Since 1791

Amendment 16 — The Income Tax

Ratified 1913 The amendment that built the modern state 4 min read

In the Text

Plain English

Congress can tax income, and it does not have to divide the tax among the states by population. Those two clauses sound technical. They are the reason the federal government looks the way it does.

The problem

Article I required that “direct taxes” be apportioned among the states according to population. If Congress wanted to raise a million dollars, each state would owe a share based on how many people lived there.

That works for a tax on land. It does not work for a tax on income, because incomes are not distributed the way populations are. A poor state with a few very rich residents would pay the same as a larger, poorer state.

Congress tried an income tax anyway in 1894. The Supreme Court struck it down the next year in Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Company, holding that a tax on income was a direct tax requiring apportionment — which meant it could not practically be imposed at all.

The fix

The Sixteenth Amendment removes apportionment from income taxes. Congress proposed it in 1909 — a decade after the Court's decision — and it was ratified in February 1913.

The phrase from whatever source derived is doing quiet work. It means wages, salaries, dividends, interest, rent, and capital gains all count. Courts have read it broadly, and it is why the American income tax reaches essentially every form of money coming in.

Ratification moved slowly for three years. It sped up in early 1913, when supporters argued that a federal income tax would let Congress lower tariffs on imported goods. Tariffs were then the main source of federal money, and they made imported products expensive for ordinary buyers.

What it made possible

Before 1913, the federal government was financed mostly by tariffs on imported goods and excise taxes on things like alcohol and tobacco. It was small, and it had no capacity to grow much.

After 1913, it could. The income tax funded the First World War, the New Deal, and the Second World War. Federal receipts went from a small share of the economy to roughly a fifth of it. Every federal program you can name — Social Security, Medicare, the military, highways, research — depends on this amendment.

Why It Matters

This is the amendment that changed the size of the American government more than any other, and it never mentions spending, programs, or policy. It just removes an obstacle.

That is worth noticing about constitutional language. A sentence about apportionment, written in the dry vocabulary of tax law, enabled the largest peacetime government in world history. The framers of 1787 had built a government that could not easily tax incomes; the amendment of 1913 unfastened that lock.

It also shows how an amendment can be the direct result of a court decision. Pollock blocked the tax. The amendment overruled Pollock. That is the ordinary way constitutional change actually happens — not from philosophy, but from a specific ruling someone needed to get around.
