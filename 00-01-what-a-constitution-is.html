What a Constitution Is

Background 4 min read

In the Text

That sentence is from the document that came before the Constitution. It is worth starting here because it shows what a constitution looks like when it is built to protect the states instead of to govern the people.

Plain English

Read that sentence slowly, because the logic is the opposite of what you might expect. The states keep everything. The national government gets only what is handed to it, explicitly, in writing.

Every power not expressly delegated stays with the state. That is the default setting: state power is the rule, and national power is the exception.

The Constitution reverses that default, as you will see on the next page. But first, the more basic question: what is a constitution, and why would anyone write one down?

Four things a constitution does

It is written. Great Britain has a constitution, but it is not a document — it is a pile of statutes, court rulings, and traditions built up over centuries. A written constitution is something you can hold, read, and point to in an argument.

It sits above ordinary law. If a city ordinance conflicts with the state constitution, the ordinance loses. If a state law conflicts with the U.S. Constitution, the state law loses. A constitution is the standard that other laws get measured against.

It creates the government and limits it in the same document. It says what offices exist, who fills them, and what they may not do. The creating and the limiting are written side by side, often in the same article.

It is hard to change. This is the part people underestimate. An ordinary law passes with a majority vote and can be repealed the same way. A constitution is designed to resist that. Changing the U.S. Constitution requires two-thirds of Congress and three-fourths of the states, which is a deliberately difficult path.

What a constitution is not

It is not a law book. It does not tell you the speed limit, the tax rate, or the drinking age. It sets up the machine and says who gets to decide those things.

It is also not a description of how things actually work. A lot of American government runs on habits, statutes, and court decisions that the Constitution never mentions. Political parties are the clearest example, and the document does not contain the word.

Why write it down at all

Because the colonists had just spent a decade arguing that unwritten limits on a king were not really limits. The whole case against King George III was that he had violated rules nobody had written down clearly enough to enforce. The obvious fix was to write the rules down.

A written constitution turns a disagreement about power into a disagreement about a text. That is not a small change. It means an argument can be settled by pointing at a sentence instead of by pointing at an army.

Why It Matters

Nearly every serious argument in American politics is, underneath, an argument about what the Constitution permits. Does Congress have the power to regulate this? Can the president refuse to spend that? Does this law violate equal protection? All three questions send people back to the same short document.

That is why the rest of this project walks through it clause by clause rather than summarizing it. The exact words carry the weight. “Necessary and proper” and “regulate commerce” are not decoration — they are the hinges the whole system turns on.

One caution before you go on. A written constitution does not enforce itself. Somebody has to decide what it means and somebody has to make the government obey. Who gets that job is one of the loudest fights in the document, and it is the reason Article III exists.
