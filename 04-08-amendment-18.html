Part Four · The Amendments Since 1791

Amendment 18 — Prohibition

Ratified 1919 Repealed 1933 5 min read

In the Text

Plain English

Four verbs: manufacture, sale, transportation, importation. Notice what is missing. Drinking was never illegal, and neither was possession.

The amendment banned the business of alcohol, not the act of consuming it. Private stocks were legal. Making wine at home for your own family was eventually permitted. Buying a bottle was not.

Congress proposed it in December 1917 and it was ratified in January 1919. It took effect one year later, in January 1920.

Why it passed

The temperance movement had been building for seventy years. Its argument was not mainly about pleasure. It was about domestic violence, workplace accidents, and poverty — alcohol as a cause of family ruin.

Several other forces converged in 1917. Anti-German sentiment during the First World War turned against German-owned breweries. Wartime rationing made grain for beer and spirits look wasteful. And the progressive movement believed government could engineer better social outcomes through law.

Prohibition also had a nativist streak. Urban immigrant communities drank; rural Protestant communities did not. For some supporters, banning alcohol was partly about whose culture would define the country.

Why it failed

Enforcement was the problem, and it was enormous. The country had roughly 100,000 legal saloons in 1920 and a federal enforcement agency of about 1,500 agents.

Drinking did not stop. It moved into speakeasies, private clubs, and homes. The price of alcohol rose, which made bootlegging enormously profitable, and that money built organized crime networks that had not existed before.

Worse, the illegal alcohol was often dangerous. Industrial alcohol was denatured with poisons to make it undrinkable, then redistilled by bootleggers who did not always remove the poison. Thousands of people died or went blind from tainted liquor.

By the early 1930s, with the Depression underway, the arguments had flipped. Legalizing alcohol meant jobs and tax revenue. In 1933 the Twenty-First Amendment repealed the Eighteenth.

Why It Matters

Prohibition is the clearest case in American history of a constitutional amendment that made things worse. It is also the only amendment ever repealed in full.

The lesson is not that the supporters were foolish. They were responding to real suffering, and they believed law could fix it. The lesson is about capacity. A rule that a large share of the population intends to break cannot be enforced, unless the government is willing to become a police state.

That problem has not gone away. It is the same argument now made about the war on drugs, and about any law that regulates a widespread private behavior. The Eighteenth Amendment is the historical test case everyone cites, from both directions.

There is also a structural point worth keeping. The amendment process makes change hard on purpose. But it does not make change permanent. A supermajority can undo what a supermajority did, and in this case it took thirteen years.
