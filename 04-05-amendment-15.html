Part Four · The Amendments Since 1791

Amendment 15 — Voting and Race

Ratified 1870 A prohibition, not a grant 5 min read

In the Text

Plain English

Read the sentence carefully. It does not give anyone the right to vote. It forbids denying the vote for one specific reason.

That distinction is the whole story of the next century.

The Constitution still let states set their own voting qualifications — property, residency, literacy, whatever the state legislature chose. The Fifteenth Amendment said that whatever those rules were, race could not be one of them.

The loopholes

States did not argue. They got creative.

Grandfather clauses. If your grandfather could vote in 1866, you were exempt from literacy tests. Since enslaved people could not vote in 1866, this exempted white voters and excluded Black ones. The Supreme Court struck these down in 1915.

Literacy tests and understanding clauses. A registrar decided whether you could read and explain a passage. The standard was applied almost entirely to Black applicants, and the same registrar passed barely literate white applicants.

White primaries. Since parties were private organizations, the argument went, they could exclude whoever they liked. In much of the South the Democratic primary was the only election that mattered. The Court closed this in 1944.

Poll taxes. A fee to vote, which fell hardest on poor voters of both races but was aimed at Black voters. Banned by the Twenty-Fourth Amendment in 1964.

Violence and intimidation. No statute addressed it, and for decades almost nothing was done about it.

What finally changed it

Not the Fifteenth Amendment by itself. The Voting Rights Act of 1965 was passed under the enforcement power in Section 2. It put federal examiners into counties with a history of discrimination. It also required those jurisdictions to get federal approval before changing voting rules.

In 2013, the Supreme Court struck down the formula that decided which jurisdictions needed that approval. Within years, several states closed polling places and tightened ID requirements. Whether those changes violate the Fifteenth Amendment is now litigated case by case, which is a slower and harder process than the one that existed before.

Why It Matters

The Fifteenth Amendment is the clearest lesson in the difference between a right and a rule against discrimination. A right to vote would have been self-executing. A ban on one kind of discrimination invited every other kind.

It took ninety-five years — from 1870 to 1965 — before the amendment's promise was actually enforced across the country. Three more amendments were needed along the way: the Nineteenth for women, the Twenty-Fourth for poll taxes, and the Twenty-Sixth for eighteen-year-olds.

That gap is the honest history. A constitutional guarantee on paper, with no enforcement mechanism and no political will behind it, changed very little for a very long time. The amendment was necessary. It was nowhere near sufficient.
